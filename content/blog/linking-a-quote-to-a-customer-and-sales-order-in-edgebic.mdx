**A quote in EDGEBIC ties to a customer and, optionally, to a sales order, and both links carry forward to the manufacturing order when the quote converts.** In EDGEBIC by User Solutions, the customer link identifies whose job the quote is, the sales-order link ties it back to an originating order when one exists, and conversion moves both onto the production order without re-keying. Getting these relationships right keeps the whole chain connected: the customer, the quote that promised them a date, and the job that delivers it.

## The customer link: record or name

Every quote is for someone, and EDGEBIC captures that in one of two ways. The preferred way is a reference to a real customer record, a single entity that carries the customer's contact and reference details. The alternative, kept for legacy support, is a free-text customer name typed directly on the quote.

The record reference is the better choice for a simple reason: it is a real entity, not just a string. Two spellings of the same company name will not group together in reporting, and a typed name has no contact detail behind it. A customer record is one thing that every quote and order can point at, so you can pull all of a customer's quotes and jobs together, and the link travels cleanly to each converted order. The free-text option is there so older data and quick entries still work, but new quotes are better served by selecting a real customer.

## The sales-order link is optional

Not every quote begins with a sales order. Some are standalone enquiries; others are part of an existing sales order. EDGEBIC treats the sales-order link as optional to match that reality. A quote can carry just a customer, or it can reference a sales order when the enquiry belongs to one. That shared reference is also what holds a set of quotes together when a customer asks for several products at once, covered in [quoting a multi-item order](/blog/quoting-a-multi-item-order-in-edgebic).

When the link is present, it does real work at conversion: the production job that results traces back to the originating sales order, so the sales, planning, and production views of the same commitment stay connected. When it is absent, nothing is lost; the quote simply stands on its own customer link.

## Both links survive a deleted sales order

A quote is a commitment record in its own right, and EDGEBIC protects it from upstream deletions. The sales-order relationship is set to clear the link on the quote rather than cascade into deleting the quote if the sales order is later removed.

So if someone deletes a sales order a quote referenced, the quote survives intact, with its product, quantity, simulation results, customer, and pricing all preserved. It simply no longer points at a sales order that no longer exists. If that quote later converts, it converts without a sales-order link, which is the honest state given the source order is gone. This is a small design choice with a large payoff: your quote history never develops holes because an upstream record was cleaned up.

## What conversion carries

Conversion is where the links prove their value. When an accepted quote becomes a manufacturing order, EDGEBIC carries both the customer link and, if present, the sales-order link onto the new order, alongside the product, quantity, dates, scheduling direction, cost with its labor and material split, markup, and unit price.

None of the customer relationship is retyped. The order knows whose job it is and which sales order it belongs to, so the margin and customer story are intact without re-querying the quote. Combined with the order's deterministic job number, which ties it to the quote it came from, the result is a connected chain: sales order to quote to production order to finished job, each linked to the same customer. The full set of carried fields is in [how a quote becomes an order](/blog/how-a-quote-becomes-an-order-in-edgebic).

## Why the connected chain matters

Keeping these links intact is what lets a shop answer questions that a disconnected quote cannot. Which jobs on the floor belong to this customer? Which sales order does this production job trace back to? How did the quoted margin for this customer hold up once the job ran? Each of these depends on the links being carried rather than retyped, so the quote, the order, and the customer stay joined through the record trail.

This is the same principle that makes quote-versus-actual analysis possible: the estimate carries to the order so predictions can be compared to reality later; see [quote versus actual hours](/blog/quote-versus-actual-hours-in-edgebic). The customer and sales-order links carry for the same reason, so the business context travels with the work.

## Getting the links clean

A few habits keep the relationships reliable. Select a real customer record rather than typing a name, so reporting groups correctly and the link carries a real entity. Attach a sales-order reference when the enquiry belongs to one, so the production job traces back. And where older quotes carry only a free-text name, reconciling them onto real customer records tidies the history so a customer's full quote-to-job story lines up. None of this changes how a quote simulates or prices; it changes how well the results connect to the rest of the business.

## The takeaway

Linking a quote to a customer and a sales order keeps the commitment connected from enquiry to finished job. Prefer a real customer record over a free-text name, attach a sales order when one applies, and rely on conversion to carry both links onto the manufacturing order without re-keying. The sales-order link even survives its source order being deleted, so your quote history never develops gaps. The result is a chain where the customer, the quote, the order, and the job all point at each other.
