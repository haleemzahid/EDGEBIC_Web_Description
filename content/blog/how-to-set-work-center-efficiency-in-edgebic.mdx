**In EDGEBIC by User Solutions, a work center's true efficiency is the utilization multiplier inside the capacity formula: available hours equal shift hours minus losses, times the instance count, times a utilization percentage.** That percentage is what turns a theoretical 8-hour shift into the hours the scheduler will actually book, and it is the single most misunderstood number in resource setup.

The word "efficiency" gets used for two different things on a shop floor, and EDGEBIC keeps them separate. One is how much of the clock a machine really produces. The other, for output-rate machines, is how fast it runs. This page covers both, and how to set each one honestly in the current build. For the full formula behind every capacity figure, read [how EDGEBIC calculates work center capacity](/blog/how-edgebic-calculates-work-center-capacity).

## Before You Start

- **The work center already exists** ([how to add a work center](/blog/how-to-add-a-work-center-in-edgebic)).
- **You have a real number in mind.** Finite-capacity practice is to load a resource to roughly 80 to 85 percent of the theoretical clock and keep the rest as buffer for breakdowns, rework, and changeover drift.
- **You know which kind of efficiency you mean:** lost time (the machine is idle part of the shift) or slow rate (the machine produces fewer pieces per hour than rated).

## What Efficiency Means to the Scheduler

For every work center, shift, and date, EDGEBIC computes:

```
available hours = (shift hours - downtime - partial-holiday overlap)
                  x number of instances
                  x utilization %
```

The utilization percentage is the efficiency term. At 80 percent, a one-instance work center on an 8-hour shift offers 6.4 schedulable hours. At 100 percent it offers 8. The scheduler bakes this number into capacity at the start of a run, so a change takes effect only on the next **Drive Schedule** pass, never retroactively on bars already placed.

One point trips up new administrators: a work center also carries a stored field labeled efficiency, but the scheduling engine does not read it when computing hours. The utilization multiplier is the operative control. If you set the stored efficiency field expecting capacity to change, nothing happens on the plan.

## Setting a Realistic Rate in the Current Build

Current EDGEBIC builds run work centers at their full clock and do not expose an editable utilization field on the work center dialog. That is deliberate: rather than hide lost time behind a single percentage, the app asks you to express it where it is visible and auditable. You have three honest levers.

### 1. Shape the Shift Hours

The most durable way to reflect a machine that produces only part of the clock is to define its shift as the productive window. If a cell loses the first 30 minutes to warm-up and the last 30 to cleanup, an 08:30 to 15:30 shift tells the truth better than an 08:00 to 16:00 shift loaded to 87.5 percent. Shift setup lives with the calendar, covered in [shifts and calendars explained](/blog/edgebic-shifts-and-calendars-explained).

### 2. Add Downtime Allowances

Recurring, predictable losses, such as a weekly lubrication run or a daily changeover window, belong in a downtime event. The scheduler subtracts the window's hours before it applies the instance and utilization math, so the loss is accounted for automatically every week. See [how to schedule a downtime event for a machine](/blog/how-to-schedule-a-downtime-event-for-a-machine-in-edgebic).

### 3. Use Capacity Overrides

For a temporary derate, a per-day or date-range capacity override sets the exact hours a machine will offer, carries a reason, and reverts when you reset it. This is the right tool for a partial-crew stretch or a machine running below spec while it waits for parts. See [how to set a work center to run at reduced capacity](/blog/how-to-set-a-work-center-to-run-at-reduced-capacity-in-edgebic).

## The Pieces Efficiency Factor (Output-Rate Machines Only)

Pieces-based work centers carry a second, genuine efficiency control: an efficiency factor on the output rate. Effective pieces per hour equals the base pieces-per-hour rate times the efficiency factor, and the factor is clamped between 0.1 and 2.0.

Use it when a line runs at a sustained rate different from its rated one. A molder rated at 120 pieces per hour that consistently holds 108 has an efficiency factor of 0.9. Set the factor to 1.0 for a machine running at spec. This factor changes output rate, not clock hours, so it belongs only on machines you schedule by pieces. For the difference between the two capacity models, read [pieces-based vs hours-based capacity](/blog/pieces-based-vs-hours-based-capacity-edgebic).

## What Changes When You Apply It

| Change | Effect on the plan |
|---|---|
| Shorter honest shift hours | Fewer schedulable hours per day at the next Drive Schedule run |
| Downtime allowance added | Recurring hours come off automatically, every matching day |
| Per-day or date-range override | Exact hours honored ahead of the shift formula, on the days it covers |
| Pieces efficiency factor changed | Output rate rescales; every job on that line takes more or less time |

Nothing moves until you rerun. Master-data edits never reschedule on their own.

## How to Check It Worked

1. Open the Resource Calendar and read the work center's available hours for a normal working day. Confirm the number matches your intended productive rate, not the gross clock.
2. Run **Drive Schedule** and watch whether jobs on that resource stretch or compress as expected.
3. For a pieces line, place a test job and confirm the duration reflects the effective rate (base rate times factor), not the base rate alone.

## Common Mistakes

**Loading every machine to 100 percent and hoping the plan absorbs the drift.** A fully loaded plan has no recovery room, so a single breakdown cascades into every downstream promise. Build the 15 to 20 percent buffer in deliberately.

**Confusing lost time with slow rate.** Halving a pieces efficiency factor doubles every job's time and models a slower machine, not an idle one. If the real problem is that the machine sits idle part of the shift, fix it with shift hours or downtime and leave the factor at 1.0.

**Expecting the stored efficiency field to throttle capacity.** It does not feed the hours formula. Use the utilization-shaping levers above.
