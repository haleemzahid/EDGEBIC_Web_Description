**A product category is a named grouping applied to a product record that organizes the catalog for browsing, filtering and reporting, without changing how anything is routed or scheduled.** It is the label that turns a flat list of several hundred part numbers into something a planner can read: raw materials in one band, sub-assemblies in another, finished goods in a third. In EDGEBIC by User Solutions the product list is grouped by category by default, so the categories you choose are the first structure anyone sees when they open the catalog.

## How it works

Categories are maintained as their own small set of records and then assigned to products one at a time or in bulk. The category record itself is short:

| Field | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Name** | The label shown in the grid band and in the product form's dropdown |
| **Code** | A short, stable code for grids and exported files |
| **Description** | Free-form notes about what belongs in the band |
| **Active** | Whether the category is still offered for new assignments |

On the product form the **Category** dropdown assigns one of them, and a product with nothing assigned reads as no category. You can create a category on the spot from beside that dropdown, which matters more than it sounds: the alternative is abandoning a half-finished product form to go and define a band first, and the shortcut is what keeps catalog hygiene from becoming an interruption.

Once assigned, categories work in three places. The product list groups by category, so the bands can be collapsed and expanded to reduce a long catalog to the part of it you care about. A toolbar filter narrows the list to one category, with an all-categories option and a clear button to reset. And reports that group by category inherit the same structure, so the way the catalog reads on screen is the way it reads on paper.

What categories deliberately do not do is behave like rules. A category never restricts which work centers a product can use, never changes a promise date, never affects capacity. The scheduler reads a product's routing steps, the work centers those steps point at, and the hours each step consumes. Nothing in that chain consults a category. That is a feature: it means you can reorganize the catalog on a Friday afternoon with zero risk to Monday's plan.

Because there is no product type switch on the form, categories carry a second job. A product's real role comes from how it is used, and the category is where you make that role legible. See [what is a procured item in manufacturing](/blog/what-is-a-procured-item-in-manufacturing) for the flag that pairs with the category on purchased stock, and [what are bill of routing status buckets](/blog/what-are-bor-status-buckets) for the column that reports whether a product has a routing at all.

## A concrete example

Watch the sample plant's catalog take shape and the categories arrive naturally rather than as an exercise.

The planner adds Widget-A first: product id Widget-A, unit of measure Each, sale price 120.00, rollup cost 48.50, a two-day delivery-ready tail. The category Finished Goods does not exist yet, so it is created from beside the dropdown in the same breath. Bracket-B follows into the same band at sale price 14.00 and rollup cost 10.00. Frame-S, a sub-assembly with a routing of its own but no sale price because it is never sold alone, goes into Sub-Assemblies. Steel-Plate-4mm, rollup cost 6.20, goes into Raw Materials with no routing planned.

Four products, three bands. The grid now shows three collapsible groups, and the routing status column reads Active BOR inside the first two bands once their routings are built, and No BOR inside Raw Materials, which is exactly right for a bought item. A planner who opens the catalog six months later, with two hundred rows in it, still reads the same three bands and still finds the mill part they were looking for inside the one that makes sense.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

Category assignment travels with the product record everywhere. The categories window handles the set itself: create with a name and code, edit, and deactivate the ones that have outlived their usefulness. The product form assigns them, the toolbar filters by them, and the grid groups by them.

The bulk path matters for any catalog beyond a couple of dozen items. A product import mask maps your file's columns onto product fields once, including a category column, so a legacy type or class field in a spreadsheet becomes a proper category band on the first run and stays current on every run after it. Matching is by product id and is not case sensitive, and blank cells on an update preserve the existing value rather than wiping it, so a partial file can safely carry category changes alone. The mechanics of that are covered in [what is an import entity type](/blog/what-is-an-import-entity-type) and [what is an upsert in data import](/blog/what-is-an-upsert-in-data-import).

Column widths, grouping and filters are remembered per user, so a planner who prefers the catalog filtered to Finished Goods finds it that way tomorrow without re-applying anything.

Two habits are worth adopting. Keep the set small and honest, because a band that holds three products earns nobody anything. And name bands after how the shop talks, not after an accounting hierarchy, since the people reading the grid are planners rather than controllers.

## The takeaway

A product category is cheap structure with real payoff: it makes a growing catalog navigable, it gives reports a natural grouping, and it costs nothing at scheduling time because it changes no behavior. Define three or four bands, assign them in bulk on the next import, and leave the routing and capacity questions to the fields that actually drive them.
