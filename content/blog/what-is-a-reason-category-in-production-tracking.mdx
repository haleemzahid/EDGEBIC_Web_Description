**A reason category is the fixed four-value grouping every reason code belongs to: machine, material, quality, or waiting.** A reason code names the specific cause an operator or planner selects when work pauses. The category is the family that code sits in, and it is what turns a long, site-specific list of codes into four comparable buckets a manager can act on. EDGEBIC by User Solutions carries a category on every reason code so lost time can be summarized without anyone having to interpret thirty individual labels.

## How it works

When work stops, somebody records why. The reason code is the specific answer: broken tap, no blanks, first article hold, waiting on drawing change. Those codes are yours to define, and a plant naturally accumulates them, because every new kind of problem tempts somebody to add a new label.

That growth is useful at the shop floor level and useless at the reporting level. Thirty codes produce thirty small percentages, and nothing in that list tells a manager where to spend the next improvement effort. The category solves this by fixing a stable layer above the codes.

**Machine** covers anything caused by the equipment itself: a failed spindle, broken tooling, a hydraulic leak. **Material** covers missing or defective input: the blanks did not arrive, the stock was out of spec. **Quality** covers holds and inspections: a first article check, a quality hold pending disposition. **Waiting** covers a pause where the operator is blocked on something outside their control that is not one of the other three: an approval, a decision, a crane, a person.

There are only four, and that is deliberate. A short fixed list means categories stay comparable across work centers, across plants, and across years, no matter how the code list underneath them evolves. Add ten new codes next quarter and the four-bucket picture still lines up with last quarter's.

The categories also point at different owners. Machine time is a maintenance conversation. Material time is a purchasing and staging conversation. Quality time is an inspection and process capability conversation. Waiting time is almost always an organizational conversation rather than a shop floor one, and it is the category most often mis-assigned, because it is tempting to log a wait against the machine that happened to be idle.

## A concrete example

Think of the drop-down a call center agent picks from when logging why a call ran long. There might be forty specific reasons in that list, and the agent needs that specificity to describe their call accurately. The operations manager reviewing a thousand calls does not want forty reasons. They want to know whether long calls are a systems problem, a training problem, a customer problem, or a policy problem, because each of those has a different owner and a different fix.

The four reason categories do the same job in a plant. On a bad week, Mill-1 logs eleven separate pause codes. Rolled up, they read: machine 2.5 hours, material 6 hours, quality 0.5 hours, waiting 1 hour. That single line changes the conversation. Nobody needs to argue about which of the eleven codes matters most, because six of the ten lost hours were material, and the fix is upstream of the machine entirely.

Reading it the other way round is the trap. If somebody had logged the material shortages against a machine code because the mill was the thing standing idle, the same week would have read as a maintenance failure and the wrong department would have spent a month chasing it.
