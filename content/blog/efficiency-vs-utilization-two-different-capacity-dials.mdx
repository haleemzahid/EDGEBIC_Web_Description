**Efficiency and utilization are two separate capacity dials in EDGEBIC by User Solutions, and confusing them is one of the most common modeling mistakes. Efficiency scales the run time of the work, because a faster or slower machine makes each piece in less or more time. Utilization scales the hours a work center makes available, because a resource planned below one hundred percent commits only part of its shift to the schedule. One changes how long the work takes; the other changes how much time there is to do it.** They compose differently, they answer different questions, and using the wrong one produces a plan that is quietly wrong.

Both dials can reduce how much a resource gets done, which is why they get mixed up. But they do it through different math, at different points in the calculation, and the fix for a too-tight plan depends on knowing which one is really binding.

## Efficiency scales the work

Efficiency is a property of the machine's speed. A work center that runs parts faster than the reference has an efficiency that shrinks run time; one that runs slower has an efficiency that stretches it. It multiplies the run hours of an operation, the per-piece work, and only the run:

```
operation run = base run hours x efficiency factor
```

A slower old mill genuinely takes longer to make each part, so its operations show more run hours than the same job on a faster new mill. That is efficiency doing its job: representing real speed differences between machines. Crucially, efficiency does not touch setup, because setup is preparation, not production, a distinction developed in [how efficiency scales run time but not setup](/blog/how-efficiency-scales-run-time-but-not-setup). A slow machine's changeover is not slower just because its run is.

Efficiency, then, is about the size of the work. Lower efficiency makes every operation on the machine bigger in run hours.

## Utilization scales the available hours

Utilization is a planning choice, not a speed. It says how much of a work center's clock time to commit to the schedule. A resource planned at eighty percent offers eighty percent of its shift hours as schedulable capacity:

```
available hours = shift hours x instances x utilization
```

The reserved twenty percent is protective headroom, held back on purpose to absorb rush jobs, breakdowns, and the variability that any real floor has. The case for not committing every hour is made in [why one hundred percent utilization is a trap](/blog/why-100-percent-utilization-is-a-trap), and the general idea of holding capacity in reserve is [protective versus productive capacity](/blog/protective-vs-productive-capacity).

Utilization is about the size of the day. Lower utilization makes the pool of hours per day smaller, so fewer job-hours fit before the day is full, without changing how long any single job takes to run.

## The two compose at different points

The reason they must be separate is that they enter the calculation at different points. Efficiency is applied to the work, per operation, when the engine computes how many run hours a job needs on a given machine. Utilization is applied to the resource, per day, when the engine computes how many hours that machine offers before any job is placed.

So the plan is built like this: utilization sets the daily pool of available hours; efficiency sets how many of those hours each operation consumes. A low utilization and a low efficiency both make the plan tighter, but one shrank the pool and the other enlarged the demand. They are not interchangeable, and folding them into one number would misapply whichever effect you did not mean, for example scaling setup by a speed factor or reserving protective time as if the machine had gotten slower.

## A worked example: 20 percent, two ways

Take a work center on an 8-hour day shift, single instance, and a job whose base run is 10 hours. Start with efficiency 1.0 and utilization 100 percent.

**Baseline.** Available hours per day: 8 x 1 x 1.0 = 8. Job run: 10 x 1.0 = 10 hours. The job fills all of day one and 2 hours of day two.

**Cut efficiency to 0.8 higher, meaning 20 percent slower (factor 1.25).** Available hours unchanged at 8. Job run: 10 x 1.25 = 12.5 hours. The job now needs a full day one and 4.5 hours of day two. The work got bigger; the day stayed the same.

**Instead, cut utilization to 80 percent.** Job run unchanged at 10 hours. Available hours: 8 x 1 x 0.8 = 6.4 per day. The job now needs day one's 6.4 hours and 3.6 hours of day two. The work stayed the same; the day got smaller.

Both changes push the finish later, but for opposite reasons and with different side effects. The efficiency change makes every estimate on that machine longer, which is correct only if the machine really is slower. The utilization change leaves estimates honest and reserves headroom, which is correct if the goal is protective slack. Pick the wrong dial and you either inflate your durations while pretending to reserve slack, or under-book the machine while costing jobs at the wrong run time.

## Which dial to reach for

Use efficiency to represent real speed differences: an older machine, a reduced-speed mode, a member of a pool that genuinely runs faster or slower. Set it to the machine's true speed and leave it there.

Use utilization to represent how much of the clock you are willing to commit: protective headroom on a busy resource, a planned load target, deliberate slack for variability. It is the dial for reserving capacity, not for modeling speed.

Keep them distinct and the plan stays honest in both directions: durations reflect how long work actually takes, and daily capacity reflects how much you have chosen to commit.
