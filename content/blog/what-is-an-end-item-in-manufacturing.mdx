**An end item is the finished product a manufacturing order is created to produce, the root of its routing, distinct from the components and materials consumed to build it.** It is what the customer ordered and what ships when the job is done. Everything else in the routing feeds toward it.

## How it works

A routing describes how to make one product. At its root is the end item, the finished thing the order exists to deliver. Below it, the routing lists the steps that build it and the inputs those steps consume: raw materials that go in, and components, which are sub-assemblies produced or bought and fitted along the way.

The distinction is about role, not importance. A material is stock consumed by a step. A component is a sub-assembly input to a step. The end item is the single output the whole routing converges on. In the recipe analogy, the flour and eggs are materials, a pre-made filling is a component, and the finished cake is the end item. One order produces one end item, however many inputs it takes.

The end item also carries the timing that the plant is judged against. After the last work-center operation, the manufactured item physically exists, but it may still need a delivery, cure, or ship tail before it is deliverable. That tail is the end item's lead time, and lateness is measured against the delivery-ready date, not the moment the last machine stops.

## A concrete example

Take a manufacturing order for 100 gearboxes. The gearbox is the end item. The routing runs through machining, sub-assembly, and final assembly. Along the way it consumes steel, a material, and fits a gear cluster and a casing, both components produced or purchased as sub-assemblies. Only the gearbox is what the order delivers; the steel and the sub-assemblies are inputs.

Now watch two things the end item anchors. First, progress. Ask how far along the job is, and the answer rolls the job's actual hours and pieces up against the single end item, the gearbox, and it excludes the sub-assembly rollup rows so their hours are not double counted. A reading of 62 percent, with 48 of 78 planned hours logged, is 62 percent of the finished gearbox, not of any one sub-assembly.

Second, timing. Suppose the final assembly comes off the line Wednesday, but the gearbox needs a two-day cure before it ships. Wednesday is the item start date, when the part exists. The delivery-ready date is Friday. The dashboard judges lateness against Friday, so finishing the machining Wednesday with a two-day tail is on plan, not late.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

In EDGEBIC, the end item is the product a manufacturing order names as its output, sitting at the root of the order's routing, above the material and component rows the routing consumes. The routing distinguishes these roles: a step's target can be a work center, a material, or a component sub-assembly, while the end item is the finished product the whole structure builds.

Two end-item behaviors show up across the product. The end-item lead time splits the item start date, when the last operation finishes, from the delivery-ready job end date, and every surface that classifies lateness uses that delivery-ready date so they all agree. A separate progress roll-up aggregates the job's operational actuals against the single end product, excluding sub-assembly and component splits, to give the canonical answer to how far along the finished item is.
