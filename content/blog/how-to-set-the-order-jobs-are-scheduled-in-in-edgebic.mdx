**To set the order jobs are scheduled in EDGEBIC, set each job's priority integer, where a lower number is planned first, and rely on start date then due date as the tie-breakers. EDGEBIC by User Solutions plans each job completely and reserves its capacity before starting the next, so on a busy plant the queue order is the plan.** Set the sequence before you run, because reworking priorities afterward moves bars twice.

This is the sequencing procedure. To set a single job's number, see [how to set a job priority in EDGEBIC](/blog/how-to-set-a-job-priority-in-edgebic); for the strategy behind it, read [job priority and sequencing strategy](/blog/job-priority-and-sequencing-strategy-edgebic). It is one of the task guides in the [EDGEBIC how-to hub](/blog/edgebic-how-to-hub).

## Before You Start

| Prerequisite | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The jobs are on the Drive Schedule grid | Priority is edited on the order rows. |
| You know your real urgency order | The queue is only as good as the priorities you feed it. |
| You plan to reschedule after | The sequence takes effect on the next run, not on save. |

## The Three-Level Sort

The engine decides sequence in a strict order:

1. **Priority**, lower number first. This is the primary lever and the one you control directly.
2. **Start date**, as the first tie-breaker. Among equal priorities, the job that may begin earlier is planned first.
3. **Due date**, as the second tie-breaker. If priority and start date both match, the job promised sooner goes first.

Because each job claims capacity as it is planned, the sequence is not cosmetic. On a loaded machine, whoever is planned first gets the good slots and everyone else fits around them.

## Set the Sequence

1. On the **Drive Schedule** tab, click directly in the **Priority** cell of a job and type a new integer. Lower means earlier.
2. Repeat for the other jobs whose order you want to control. You do not need unique numbers; jobs that share urgency can share a priority and let the date tie-breakers order them.
3. Click **Save Changes**, the button that highlights once you have unsaved edits.
4. Tick the affected jobs and press **Schedule + Re-Schedule** so the new sequence is applied.

If your grid supports drag-to-reorder, dragging a row resets its priority value to match the new position, which is the same lever expressed visually.

## What Changes When You Run

| Surface | Effect |
|---|---|
| The queue | Jobs are planned in priority, then start-date, then due-date order. |
| Capacity | Each job reserves its hours before the next is planned, so earlier jobs get first pick. |
| Later jobs | Fit around the reservations the earlier jobs already made. |
| Days Late | Can improve for a promoted job and worsen for a demoted one, which is the trade-off you are making. |

## A Worked Sequence

Take one day shift, 08:00 to 16:00, one saw and one mill, no other load.

| Job | Step 1 | Step 2 | Due |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bracket, priority 1 | Saw 2 h | Mill 8 h | Wed |
| Plate, priority 2 | Saw 6 h | Mill 2 h | Fri |

With the bracket at priority 1, it is planned first: saw Monday 08:00 to 10:00, then mill Monday 10:00 to 16:00 and Tuesday 08:00 to 10:00. The plate follows on the saw from 10:00 and reaches the mill Tuesday at 10:00. Both land on time and the mill runs with no idle gap.

Swap the priorities and rerun. The plate now holds the saw until 14:00, the bracket's cut finishes only at 16:00 Monday, and its eight mill hours consume all of Tuesday. Same orders, same capacity, a different queue, and the bracket's promise survives with zero margin. That is why the sequence belongs before the run.

## How to Check It Worked

1. On the Gantt, confirm the job you promoted claimed the shared machine before the others.
2. Read the **Days Late** column across the affected jobs and confirm the trade-off matches what you intended.
3. Re-open a priority cell and confirm the saved value is the one you typed.
4. If two equal-priority jobs ordered surprisingly, check their start dates and due dates, which are the tie-breakers doing their job.

## Common Mistakes and Gotchas

- **Editing priority without saving.** Inline edits light up a Save Changes button. Navigate away without clicking it and the edits are lost.
- **Forgetting to reschedule.** The new sequence only takes effect on the next run. Save, then run.
- **Setting priorities after scheduling.** Re-running to fix the order moves bars twice. Decide the sequence first.
- **Giving every job a unique number.** You do not have to. Equal priorities let the start-date and due-date tie-breakers order genuinely equal jobs meaningfully.

A word on when the sequence stops mattering. On a lightly loaded plant with plenty of open capacity, priority barely shows, because every job finds a slot regardless of order. The moment a machine fills up, the sequence becomes the plan: whoever is planned first takes the good hours and everyone else waits. That is exactly when a few minutes spent setting priorities before the run pays off, and exactly when re-running to fix the order afterward is most expensive, because the bars move twice and the shop floor sees churn. Set the queue deliberately on a loaded plant and let it stand.
