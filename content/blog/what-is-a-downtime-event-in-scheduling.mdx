**A downtime event is a defined period when a single work center is unavailable, whether from planned maintenance, a changeover, or an unexpected breakdown.** It removes capacity from that one machine for the window you enter, so the engine will not place any job on it during that time. Unlike a holiday, which closes work by the calendar, a downtime event attaches a reason and a specific window to one resource. EDGEBIC by User Solutions uses downtime events so a machine coming offline, for any reason, is reflected in the schedule instead of being a surprise on the floor.

## How it works

Every downtime event has a type, and the type tells you whether the schedule could see it coming. Scheduled downtime is planned in advance: a monthly press calibration on the first Monday, a coolant change, a preventive-maintenance slot. Because you know it ahead of time, you enter it before it happens and the schedule already avoids it. Unscheduled downtime is the surprise: a spindle bearing that fails mid-shift, a hydraulic leak, an unplanned repair. You enter it after the fact, then reschedule so the plan catches up to reality.

Either way, the effect on capacity is the same. During the downtime window, the affected work center has no available hours. When the engine schedules, it treats those hours as gone and walks forward to the next opening, exactly as it does when a machine is simply fully booked. If the step has alternate work centers, the engine can also move the work onto a substitute rather than waiting for the original machine to come back.

Because a downtime event targets one work center for one window, it is more surgical than a holiday. You can block just the six hours a repair takes, on just the machine involved, without touching any other resource or the rest of the day.

## A concrete example

Picture the machine log the way a garage tracks a lift. A garage might block one lift on Saturday from eight in the morning until two in the afternoon for a hydraulic repair. That single lift is out; the other bays keep taking cars. That is a downtime event: one resource, one window, a reason attached.

If instead the whole garage closed for a public holiday, no bay would run at all. That is the calendar closing work, not a downtime event. And if the garage knew in advance that the lift needed its annual inspection on the first Monday of every month, it would pencil that in ahead of time rather than discovering it when a car was already up in the air. Scheduled versus unscheduled downtime is exactly that difference: one is on the calendar before the fact, the other is recorded after the breakdown.

In the plant, "CNC number three offline Saturday eight to two for spindle replacement" is a downtime event. The machine loses those hours, the rest of the plant runs, and any operation that was sitting in that window moves forward on the next reschedule.

Downtime events are entered against the work center they affect, with a window and a type.
