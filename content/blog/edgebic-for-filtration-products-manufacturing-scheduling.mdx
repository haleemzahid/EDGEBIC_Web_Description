**A filter plant rarely runs out of capacity, it runs out of synchronization.** EDGEBIC by User Solutions overlaps media converting with pleating using transfer batches, pools pleaters so runs land on a free machine, and carries media grade changeovers as real sequence-dependent cost.

Converting, pleating, potting, and assembly are all fast operations. The elapsed time is spent waiting for the previous step to finish entirely before the next one is allowed to start.

## The Handoff Is the Problem

The classic filtration schedule runs each step to completion before releasing the next. Converting produces a full run of media, then pleating starts, then assembly starts. Nothing is wrong with any individual step and the whole thing still takes three times longer than it needs to.

Set a transfer batch size on the converting step. Pleating then begins as soon as the first sublot of media is ready rather than waiting for the whole run. If converting produces 5,000 square feet and the transfer batch is 500, pleating starts after the first 500. The two operations overlap on the schedule because the schedule now knows they are allowed to. See [how to set a transfer batch size](/blog/how-to-set-a-transfer-batch-size-in-edgebic).

Set a second transfer batch on pleating so assembly starts after the first tray of packs rather than the full lot. The compounding effect across three handoffs is where most of the elapsed time comes back.

Two cautions. The transfer batch must be a quantity the floor can actually move and stage, and it must respect any lot integrity or traceability rule your quality system imposes. A transfer batch that exists only in the plan produces a schedule the floor cannot follow.

## Media Grade Changeovers

A filter plant with a hundred SKUs usually has ten or twelve media grades behind them, and the grade change is where the hours go. Two grades sharing a base substrate may cost a short splice and a threading run. A move to a different substrate, or to a cleanroom-grade media that requires a purge run, costs far more.

Build a setup matrix on the converting and pleating lines. Group the grades into four or five families and record family-to-family changeover hours: five families gives you twenty-five cells rather than a hundred and forty-four grade-to-grade entries, which is what makes the table maintainable. See [how setup families reduce scheduling complexity](/blog/how-setup-families-reduce-scheduling-complexity).

With real numbers in the matrix, the sequence optimizer groups compatible grades on the constrained line. The multi-run layer is guaranteed never to return a schedule worse than the baseline it started from, so running it carries no downside: you compare total changeover hours and late-job count and accept only if it wins.

## Pooling the Pleaters

Most plants run several pleaters and the traveler usually names one because that is how the SKU was set up. Every run then queues behind every other run on that machine while an identical pleater sits open.

Bind the pleating step to a work center group instead. At schedule time the engine expands the group into its members, compares each member's projected finish against live load, and places the run on the pleater that gets it done first. Members carry their own effective rate, so a rotary pleater that runs faster than a blade pleater is modeled honestly rather than averaged. Details are in [how a work center group shops a pool of machines](/blog/how-a-work-center-group-shops-a-pool-of-machines).

If one pleater is the only one that handles a particular pleat depth or media stiffness, leave that work bound to it and pool only what is genuinely interchangeable.

## Cure and Potting as Elapsed Time

Endcap potting, adhesive cure, and urethane set are elapsed time, not worked time. A four-hour cure started at 3 PM finishes at 7 PM whether or not the shift ends at 4. Put the cure on the routing as its own step with the real elapsed window and the downstream assembly is timed off when the parts are genuinely ready.

If cure runs in an oven with limited racks, model the oven as its own work center with real capacity so two lots cannot be promised the same window.

## Scheduling in Pieces Rather Than Hours

A converting line thinks in square feet and a pleater thinks in packs per hour. Configure those work centers to schedule in pieces with their own throughput rate, and put the required quantity on the routing step. The engine converts quantity to elapsed time using each machine's own rate, so a faster line genuinely finishes sooner in the plan. See [how hours and pieces convert on the floor](/blog/how-hours-and-pieces-convert-on-the-floor).

## A Worked Order

A 2,000-unit cartridge filter order through a plant with three pleaters.

| Step | Resource | Time | Placed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Convert media | Converting line, same grade family as prior run | 6 hours plus 0.5h splice | Mon 7 AM to 1:30 PM |
| Pleat | Pleater group member picked at schedule time | 4 hours, starts after first sublot | Mon 8:30 AM to 12:30 PM |
| Pack and seam | Seaming cell, starts after first tray | 3 hours | Mon 10 AM to 1 PM |
| Pot endcaps | Potting cell | 2 hours | Mon 1 PM to 3 PM |
| Cure | Cure oven, elapsed | 4 hours | Mon 3 PM to 7 PM |
| Test and pack | Integrity test stand | 2 hours | Tue 7 AM to 9 AM |

Run sequentially, converting through potting alone is fifteen hours and lands Tuesday afternoon. With transfer batches on the first two handoffs, the same work finishes potting Monday at 3 PM and ships Tuesday morning. The pleating landed on Pleater 2 because Pleater 1 was still committed to a Monday morning run.

## Traceability Without Losing the Overlap

Filtration often carries lot traceability requirements, and transfer batches can look like a threat to them. They are not, provided each sublot is identified and the schedule records which sublot went where. Because operators log start, stop, and quantity from the floor, the record of what ran and when is captured as it happens, and completed operations are never moved by a reschedule. See [how to log actual hours and pieces](/blog/how-to-log-actual-hours-and-pieces-in-edgebic).
