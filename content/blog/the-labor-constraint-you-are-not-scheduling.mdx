**The labor constraint is the one most schedules ignore: they check whether the machine is free and assume a qualified person is standing at it.** EDGEBIC by User Solutions can treat people as a finite resource, so a routing step that names a required skill is scheduled only into a window where a certified, rostered, present operator still has unbooked hours. That single rule closes the gap where a plant with one certified welder gets shown five simultaneous jobs and nobody notices until the shift starts.

This post is about the delivery and trust outcome of modeling labor. For the full model, see [operator and skill scheduling](/blog/edgebic-operator-and-skill-scheduling-explained). This post sits under the [EDGEBIC results guide](/blog/edgebic-results-guide).

## The Failure Machine-Only Scheduling Cannot See

A machine-only scheduler asks one question: does this work center have free hours? If Weld-A and Weld-B both show eight free hours on Monday, two Inconel jobs both get Monday. The plan prints, the supervisor prints it, and at 08:00 one certified welder discovers he is expected in two places.

Nothing there is a software defect. The scheduler answered the question it was asked. The problem is that the question was incomplete, and the cost is silent slip. The plan degrades quietly, dates move without anyone deciding they should, and after a few weeks the floor stops trusting the printed schedule and runs from memory. Skilled-labor availability is one of the constraints that separates a plan from a wish, a standard concern in [job shop labor scheduling](/blog/job-shop-labor-scheduling).

## Modeling a Person as Real Capacity

With the labor constraint configured, [EDGEBIC](/edgebic) asks both questions at once: is the machine available, and is there a qualified person with hours left to stand at it. The model has six parts:

| Piece | What it is |
|---|---|
| Skill | A named certification such as TIG-Weld or CMM Inspection. A routing step requires exactly one |
| Operator | A shop-floor person with a name, a badge or payroll code, and an active flag |
| Certification | The link between an operator and a skill, with an optional date, expiry, and proficiency |
| Roster | A weekly pattern: which operator works which shift on which weekday |
| Time off | Absence ranges, the person's version of a work center holiday |
| Step labor fields | The required skill, operator attention, and an optional pin to one named person |

Two design choices explain most of the behavior. **People carry hours, not presence:** each operator holds a budget of hours per shift per day, so a person certified in three skills still has one budget, and booking them through welding leaves fewer hours for inspection. A per-skill pool would sell the same person into three pools at once, which is the double-count in a new costume. And **the roster is a weekly pattern with absences as exceptions**, which mirrors how plants actually staff. See [how EDGEBIC books operator hours](/blog/how-edgebic-books-operator-hours) for the arithmetic.

## Machine and Human Compose on One Step

A routing step can carry both a machine target and a labor requirement, and they answer different questions. The work center, or the [work center group](/blog/edgebic-work-center-groups-explained), answers which machine. The required skill answers which qualified person. The step schedules only when both answers exist in the same window, which is exactly what [operator skills constraining a schedule](/blog/how-operator-skills-constrain-a-schedule) enforces.

That composition is the whole outcome. The machine constraint and the labor constraint are checked together, so the plan cannot promise a machine you have while quietly assuming a person you do not.

## The Outcome, Measured Two Ways

Modeling labor pays back on two numbers that shops feel immediately.

**Fewer silent slips.** The double-booking that used to surface at shift start now surfaces at planning time, weeks earlier, as a job that simply did not fit the qualified operator's hours. You resolve it with a decision (train a second operator, move the date, add a shift) instead of a scramble. That is the difference between managing capacity and reacting to it, the same shift described in [ending the daily firefight](/blog/how-finite-capacity-scheduling-reduces-firefighting).

**A schedule the floor believes.** When the plan stops promising one welder to two jobs, the floor stops overriding it. Trust is the quiet return here, and it compounds: a schedule people follow is a schedule that produces the on-time numbers it predicted. For why that trust is fragile and how it is earned, see [getting the floor to trust the schedule](/blog/getting-your-team-to-trust-the-schedule-edgebic).

## What the Labor Constraint Cannot Do

**It cannot train operators.** The schedule shows you that one certified welder is your constraint and that his hours are the ceiling on Inconel work. Deciding to certify a second person is a management call. The plan makes the case with weeks of warning; it does not make the hire.

**It cannot cover a roster you never entered.** If the certifications, rosters, and time off are not maintained, the constraint is blind, and the plan reverts to machine-only behavior. The model is only as honest as the roster behind it, which is why maintaining it is part of the daily rhythm, not a one-time setup.

**It cannot decide that a skill requirement is optional.** If a step names a required skill, the scheduler treats it as mandatory. Loosening that (allowing an uncertified hand under supervision, say) is a process decision you make by editing the step, not something the scheduler guesses.

**It cannot model a person as being in two places by working faster.** Hours are hours. If the work genuinely needs more qualified-person hours than exist, the plan will show the shortfall rather than hide it, and that visibility is the point.
