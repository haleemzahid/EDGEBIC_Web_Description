**Deleting a scenario removes that scenario and the overrides stored on it, and nothing else.** In EDGEBIC by User Solutions the parent quote, the product's routing, your work centers, and the live production schedule are all untouched by the delete. That makes pruning a quote's scenario list one of the safest housekeeping jobs in the product, and it is worth doing, because a quote carrying a dozen half-explored what-ifs is a quote nobody can read.

## Before you start

Know two things before you remove anything.

**Know which scenario you actually sent.** Open the quote and read its estimated end date and cost, then match those figures against the scenario list. Applying a scenario copies its results onto the quote, so the numbers on the quote tell you which scenario won. That one is your decision record.

**Know that a scenario cannot be rebuilt from memory.** A scenario stores parameters and the results of its last run. Running it again days later works against a shop that has moved on, so the same overrides can return a different date. Deleting is not reversible by re-creating.

## How to delete a scenario

1. On the **Quote** tab, select the quote and click **Scenarios** to open the **What-If Scenario Analysis** view.
2. In the scenario list on the left, select the scenario you want to remove.
3. Use **Delete Scenario**.
4. Repeat for each scenario you are pruning, then use **Back to Quotes** to return to the grid.

That is the whole procedure. There is no separate cleanup step, because a scenario owns nothing outside itself.

## What survives the delete

This is the part worth internalizing, because planners hesitate here and leave clutter behind.

| Item | Effect of deleting the scenario |
|---|---|
| The scenario's parameters and overrides | Removed. This is all the delete takes. |
| The parent quote | Untouched, including its status, price, and markup. |
| Estimated dates, hours, and cost already applied to the quote | Untouched. Once applied, those figures belong to the quote. |
| The product's routing | Untouched. A scenario never edited the routing to begin with. |
| Work centers and their real capacity | Untouched. A scenario capacity override lives inside the scenario only. |
| The production schedule | Untouched. Scenario runs write nothing to real jobs. |
| Other scenarios on the same quote | Untouched. Scenarios do not reference each other. |

The reason the blast radius is this small is the same reason scenarios are useful in the first place. A scenario is a named set of altered conditions plus the stored output of running them. It never reserved capacity, never edited a machine, and never placed a job. Read the wider boundary in [what a scenario can and cannot change in EDGEBIC](/blog/what-a-scenario-can-and-cannot-change-in-edgebic).

## How to check it worked

Three checks, in order.

1. **The scenario list is shorter** and the scenario you removed is gone from the left panel.
2. **The quote still reads the same.** Go back to the quote grid and confirm the estimated start, end, hours, and cost are exactly what they were. If you deleted a scenario you had applied, this is the check that proves applying wrote the numbers onto the quote permanently.
3. **The comparison still draws.** Open the **Comparison Analysis** tab and tick the scenarios you kept. The lead time, cost, and utilization charts should render one bar per ticked scenario, with the deleted one simply absent. Only simulated scenarios can be included, so if a survivor will not tick, run its simulation rather than assuming the delete broke something.

## Common mistakes

**Deleting the baseline.** The no-override scenario is the least interesting to look at and the most important to keep. Without it a comparison has no zero point, and you lose the record of what doing nothing would have cost. Keep it until the quote is closed.

**Deleting the applied scenario too early.** The quote keeps the numbers, but it does not keep the reasoning. Six weeks on, when a customer asks why you promised that date, the scenario named after its lever is the answer. Delete probes freely; keep the winner until the quote converts or dies.

**Treating delete as a fix for a stale result.** If a scenario's numbers look wrong because you changed its parameters and did not re-run it, the fix is to run the simulation again, not to delete and rebuild. Applying an unsimulated or edited scenario is refused with a message telling you to simulate first, which is the system pointing at the real problem.

**Letting the list grow instead of pruning as you go.** Two to four scenarios per quote is the working range: a baseline plus one per lever you would genuinely execute. Beyond that the comparison charts get crowded and the names stop meaning anything. Delete a probe when it loses, not in a cleanup pass six weeks later. Naming each scenario after its lever rather than its number makes the pruning decision obvious.

## Next steps

Once the list is down to a baseline and a winner, the remaining work is on the quote, not the scenarios. If you sold a second shift on the mill, someone still has to arrange it. ## The takeaway

Delete a scenario the moment it stops earning its place. The operation removes that scenario and its overrides and touches nothing else, so there is no risk to the quote, the routing, the work centers, or the schedule. Keep the baseline, keep the scenario you sent, and delete the probes as they lose.
