**An operator pin in EDGEBIC by User Solutions hard-assigns a routing step to one named, qualified person, so the scheduler staffs that step only from their hours and never falls back to anyone else.** It is the planner's way of saying "this operation must be run by this person," and its defining behavior is that a pinned step slides through the pinned person's vacation rather than quietly handing the work to a substitute. This is a labor-constraint feature that ships and works today, and it lives alongside the wider [shop floor execution](/blog/edgebic-shop-floor-guide) model.

Most scheduling decisions are about capacity: is there a machine and a qualified person free? A pin is different. It is about identity. Some operations genuinely need a specific individual, whether because a customer requires that person's sign-off, or because one hand is the only one you trust with a particular fixture. The pin makes that requirement explicit and enforces it honestly.

## Pin Is Not Kiosk Login

First, a clarification that saves confusion. The shop-floor kiosk has [no login, PIN, or badge scan](/blog/why-the-edgebic-kiosk-has-no-login): its Operator box is a trusted free-text name. The operator pin described here is a completely separate, planner-side scheduling concept. It does not gate who can type at a terminal. It tells the scheduling engine which named, qualified person must staff a routing step. Keep the two ideas apart and everything below is straightforward.

## How a Pin Is Set

A pin lives on the routing step, next to its required skill. In the routing editor, once a step has a required skill, an Operator picker becomes available, and it shows only operators qualified for that skill. Choosing a person pins the step to them; leaving it at Auto lets the scheduler staff from the whole qualified pool.

There is a second way to pin: on the Schedule View Gantt in Operator view, dragging a bar onto a person's lane pins the step to that person, checked against their qualification. Dragging to the Unassigned lane clears the pin. Both paths write the same result. For the drag mechanics, see [reassigning an operator with a replacement drag](/blog/reassigning-an-operator-with-a-replacement-drag).

## The Hard Semantics

A pin is deliberately strict, because a soft pin that quietly substitutes people would defeat its own purpose:

- **One person, no fallback.** With a pin set, only that person's hours count for the step. The rest of the qualified pool is never used, even when a colleague is free.
- **It slides through absence.** If the pinned person is on time off, the step moves to their first day back. Their vacation becomes visible schedule movement, not a silent hand-off.
- **It fails fast on a bad pin.** If the pinned person is inactive or not certified for the step's skill, the run stops immediately with a message naming the problem, rather than scanning weeks of calendar and ending in a vague no-capacity error.
- **It survives reschedules.** The pin is written to both the global routing and the job's preserved routing snapshot in one save, so a preserved-routing reschedule cannot silently revert it.

## Why Sliding Beats Substituting

The instinct to "just use someone else when the pinned person is out" is exactly the behavior a pin exists to prevent. If a customer requires a named welder's sign-off, or a specific machinist is the only one trusted with a fixture, substituting quietly produces a plan that violates the very requirement the pin encoded. By sliding the step to the pinned person's return, EDGEBIC keeps the plan truthful: the date moves, the planner sees it move, and no one downstream is misled into thinking the work ran with the wrong hands. Use pins sparingly, though; the Auto setting load-balances across everyone qualified and gives the scheduler more room to hold dates.

## A Worked Example

Weld-A runs a step that requires the TIG-Inconel skill. Joe and Mia are both certified and rostered Monday through Friday.

**Unpinned (Auto).** If Joe's Monday hours are booked, the scheduler staffs the step with Mia and it runs Monday. Capacity, not identity, drives the choice.

**Pinned to Joe.** Now suppose the customer requires Joe's sign-off, so the step is pinned to him, and Joe takes time off Monday through Wednesday.

- The qualified pool for the step narrows to Joe alone; Mia is filtered out of every consideration.
- Monday through Wednesday, Joe's available hours are zero, so the step cannot be placed.
- The step slides to Thursday, Joe's first day back, and runs there.

The slip is visible on the Gantt, which is the feature working. Mia's free Monday hours are not used, because the pin means "this person, visibly, or wait."

## The Clear Rule

A pin is cleared only when the required skill is removed from the step entirely, never on a skill change from one to another. Changing a step's skill keeps the pin. If the pinned person does not hold the new skill, the next run fails fast and names the mismatch, so you fix it on purpose. This rule exists because property updates during import and data loading happen in an uncontrolled order, and a clear-on-any-change rule would wipe legitimate pins during innocent processing. The shipped behavior is safe: only a deliberate "skill set to none" clears the pin.

## Where the Pin Shows Up

A pinned or skill-required step carries a person glyph on the Schedule View Gantt and a person icon beside its name in the Job View, with a required-skill column. The [Dispatch tab](/blog/how-supervisors-use-the-edgebic-dispatch-view) shows the current plan's crew per person and date. These crew surfaces show the plan, not a record of who actually worked; that record still comes from kiosk punches and actual dates. For how skills themselves gate placement, see [how operator skills gate work center assignment](/blog/how-operator-skills-gate-work-center-assignment).

## The Bottom Line

The operator pin ties a routing step to one named, qualified person and enforces it hard: no fallback, a visible slide through their absence, a fast failure on a bad pin, and survival across reschedules. It is a planner-side scheduling constraint, entirely separate from the kiosk's trusted free-text name, and it exists so that "this person must run this" is a promise the plan actually keeps. Used sparingly, it trades a little flexibility for certainty about who.
