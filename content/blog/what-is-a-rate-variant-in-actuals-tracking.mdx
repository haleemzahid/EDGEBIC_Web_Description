**A rate variant is one candidate pieces-per-hour rate an operation can use when converting between hours and pieces during actuals entry. The list of candidates always contains the routing-derived rate that the schedule planned with, plus one entry for every distinct rate observed on a day where both hours and pieces were logged. Selecting a variant pins it to that operation, and future automatic conversions use the pinned rate instead of the routing estimate.**

The idea is simple: the routing's rate is a forecast, and the floor produces evidence. A rate variant lets you switch from the forecast to the evidence for the rest of a job, without editing the routing that every other job depends on.

## How Rate Variants Work

Actuals entry can accept hours, pieces, or both. When only one is supplied, the system fills in the other by conversion, and a conversion needs a rate. Log four hours with no pieces and the rate decides the piece count; log 200 pieces with no hours and the same rate decides the hours.

The candidate list is built from two sources.

**The routing-derived rate.** This comes from the routing step's cycle time and the work center's efficiency, and it is the rate the schedule itself planned with. It is always present, always first, and is labeled as the scheduled rate. It is also the fallback whenever nothing is pinned.

**Observed rates.** Every day the operation has logged both hours and pieces produces a real ratio. One entry appears for each distinct ratio, tagged with the date that produced it. These are the rates the job actually ran at.

Selecting one and applying it pins it to that operation. It must be a positive number. Selecting the scheduled row again clears the pin and returns the operation to the routing default.

## Why This Exists at All

A routing rate is set once and used by every job that runs the routing. Editing it because one job is running slow changes the plan for every future job as well, which is usually not what a planner wants in the middle of a run.

A rate variant is scoped to the single operation, so it fixes the conversion where it is wrong without touching anything else. The routing rate stays whatever it is, and if the job's evidence turns out to be typical rather than exceptional, updating the routing afterward becomes a deliberate, informed decision rather than a mid-shift reflex.

There is one presentation detail worth knowing. If the day that produced your pinned rate is later edited, that ratio may no longer exist in the list. Rather than dropping your choice quietly, the picker keeps showing the pinned rate as active but with no source date, which is the visible signal that it is still in force and no longer anchored to a day's evidence.

## A Concrete Example

An operation is planned at 35 pieces per hour: 500 pieces in a little over fourteen hours.

Monday, the operator logs six hours and 180 pieces. That is 30 pieces per hour, not 35. Tuesday, the operator logs eight hours and 244 pieces, which is 30.5. The job is consistently running about fifteen percent under the estimate, most likely because of the material batch.

On Wednesday the operator logs five hours and no piece count. With no variant pinned, the automatic conversion uses the routing rate of 35 and fills in 175 pieces, which is roughly twenty more than the job has been managing.

The planner opens the rate picker on that operation. Three candidates appear: the scheduled 35, the Monday observation of 30, and the Tuesday observation of 30.5. They pick 30.5 and apply it. Wednesday's five hours now convert to about 152 pieces, matching how the job is genuinely running, and every later automatic conversion on this operation uses the same rate.

When the job closes, the accumulated evidence is a good argument for revisiting the routing's cycle time for that product. That is a separate, deliberate change.

## How EDGEBIC Uses Rate Variants

In EDGEBIC, the picker lives in the Log Actuals dialog, next to the automatic calculation controls. Open the rate dropdown to see the candidates, select one, and apply it. The scheduled row is always there to return to.

Two things it does not do are worth stating. It does not rewrite entries that were already saved, so a conversion that produced a wrong piece count before you pinned a rate still needs correcting by hand. And it does not change the schedule: the operation's planned hours and its place on the board are unaffected, because a rate variant governs the conversion between hours and pieces, not the capacity the plan reserved.

The neighboring definitions are the [daily hour breakdown](/blog/what-is-a-daily-hour-breakdown) whose rows produce the observed rates, [pieces per hour](/blog/what-is-pieces-per-hour-in-work-center-capacity) which is the underlying unit, the [actual entry mode](/blog/what-is-an-actual-entry-mode-in-shop-floor-tracking) that decides whether a terminal leads with hours or with pieces, and [work center efficiency](/blog/what-is-work-center-efficiency-in-scheduling) which scales the routing-derived rate. For the mechanism, see [how hours and pieces convert on the floor](/blog/how-hours-and-pieces-convert-on-the-floor), and for the practical walkthrough, [how to log actual hours and pieces](/blog/how-to-log-actual-hours-and-pieces-in-edgebic).

An estimate that has been contradicted by two days of real production is not the best number available. The rate picker is how you use the better one.
