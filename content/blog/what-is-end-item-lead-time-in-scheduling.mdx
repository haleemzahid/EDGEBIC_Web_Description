**End-item lead time is the delivery, cure, or ship tail that follows the last work-center operation of a job, measured in calendar days: it separates the moment the item physically exists from the delivery-ready date, and it consumes no work-center capacity.** It is the two quiet days between a part coming off the final machine and landing on the customer's dock, modeled honestly without pretending those days are a machine operation.

## Why a Job Needs a Tail

A part is rarely shippable the instant it leaves the last machine. Paint has to cure. Adhesive has to set. Finished goods have to travel from the plant to the customer. That gap between "off the machine" and "ready for the customer" is real time the schedule must account for, but it is not machine time: no work center is busy while paint dries or a truck rolls.

Modeling that gap as a fake operation would be wrong, because it would consume capacity that is not actually used and distort machine load. End-item lead time is the honest alternative: a calendar delay attached to the product, not a step on a machine.

## Two Dates, One Tail

End-item lead time splits a job's finish into two distinct dates:

- The **item start date** is the end of the last work-center operation. This is the moment the manufactured item physically exists, and it is when the lead-time clock starts.
- The **job end date** is the item start date plus the end-item lead time, applied forward in calendar days. This is the delivery-ready date.

When lead time is zero, the two dates coincide. When it is nonzero, the job end date sits later by exactly the tail. Critically, lateness is always judged against the **job end date**, so the dashboard, the Gantt, and the days-late figure all agree on whether a job made its promise.

On the Gantt, the tail renders as a display-only muted bar from the item start date to the job end date. It marks the delivery window visually while occupying no machine and blocking no capacity.

## A Concrete Example

A product carries a 2-day end-item lead time for paint curing. A job's final machining operation finishes Wednesday at 4 PM.

The item start date is Wednesday 4 PM: the part now exists. The job end date is Friday: two calendar days of cure later, the part is delivery-ready. If the customer due date is Friday, the job is on time, even though the last machine stopped Wednesday. The dashboard classifies it against the Friday job end date, and the Gantt shows a muted tail bar stretching Wednesday to Friday so the cure window is visible to anyone reading the plan.

## How EDGEBIC Uses It

In EDGEBIC, end-item lead time is a product-level value in calendar days that models the delivery, cure, or ship tail after a job's last operation. It creates the item-start-date to job-end-date split, drives the muted lead-time tail on the Gantt, and is the date every surface uses to classify a job as late, on time, or early.

Lead time also shapes just-in-time planning. When a job schedules backward, the engine subtracts the lead time from the due date to form the [due anchor](/blog/what-is-a-due-anchor-in-backward-scheduling), so manufacturing finishes early enough for the tail to land the part on the due date. Distinct from a lead-time tail, which happens after the last step, is [transit time](/blog/what-is-transit-time-in-production-scheduling), which is a delay between two steps mid-routing. For the full backward-scheduling mechanism and how the tail is reserved in front of the due date, see the [backward scheduling overview](/blog/edgebic-backward-scheduling-explained).
