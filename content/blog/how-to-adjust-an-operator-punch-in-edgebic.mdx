**To adjust an operator punch in EDGEBIC by User Solutions, open the punch that is wrong, enter the corrected value and a reason, and save the adjustment.** The correction keeps the original value beside the new one and stamps the supervisor who made it, so the recorded hours are put right while the change stays fully traceable. It is a supervisor-level action, separate from the live capture operators do at the kiosk, and it does not replan the schedule.

This page covers correcting one punch. For the record it corrects and why the before and after are both kept, read [what is a punch adjustment](/blog/what-is-a-punch-adjustment), and for the raw capture it works on, see [what is a punch type in shop floor tracking](/blog/what-is-a-punch-type-in-shop-floor-tracking).

## Before You Start

- You are acting as a supervisor. Correcting a punch after the fact is a supervisor action; operators capture their own punches live and do not edit them.
- You know the correct value. Have the real start time, end time, or piece count in hand, from the operator or the job, before you open the punch.
- You have a short reason ready. The adjustment records why the change was made, so a one-line reason such as "missed clock-out" or "wrong job selected" is part of the record.

## Step by Step

1. Open the punch history for the machine or the operator whose record is wrong. This is the list of captured punches, each a segment of time tagged with what the operator was doing.
2. Select the punch that needs correcting, for example the one that auto-closed at midnight when the operator forgot to end it.
3. Choose the adjust action on that punch.
4. Enter the corrected value: the real end time, start time, or piece count. The original value stays attached; you are not overwriting it.
5. Enter a reason for the change.
6. Save. The adjustment is recorded with the original value, the corrected value, the reason, and your name as the author.

## What Changes When You Save

Saving records the adjustment, not a silent overwrite. The corrected value becomes the effective figure for reporting, payroll, and job costing, while the original stays attached so a later reviewer can see a correction happened.

| Surface | Effect |
|---|---|
| The punch record | Shows the corrected value as effective, with the original preserved beside it |
| Reporting and costing | Use the corrected hours or pieces, not the erroneous capture |
| The audit trail | Records the before, the after, the reason, and the supervisor who made the change |
| The production schedule | Unchanged; correcting a punch is not a reschedule |

Because the correction is evidence rather than an erasure, nobody reviewing the day has to guess whether a number is original or amended. The adjustment carries both.

## How to Check It Worked

Reopen the punch and confirm the corrected value now reads as effective and that the original value is still visible alongside it, with your name and the reason recorded. The runaway hours or the mistyped count should be gone from the effective figure while the history of the fix remains. If you only see the new number with no trace of the original, you edited rather than adjusted; use the adjust action so the record keeps both values.

## Common Mistakes

- **Overwriting instead of adjusting.** A bare edit throws away the evidence that a correction happened. Always use the adjust action so the original value, the reason, and your name are preserved.
- **Leaving the reason blank.** The reason is what makes the correction reviewable months later. A one-line note such as "missed clock-out" costs seconds and saves an audit question.
- **Expecting the plan to move.** A punch adjustment corrects the captured actuals; it does not replan. If the corrected hours must reach the schedule, that flows through the normal actuals and reschedule path, where completed work is preserved.
- **Letting operators self-correct.** Live capture is the operator's job; after-the-fact correction is the supervisor's, precisely so the fix carries an accountable name.

## Worked Example: The Forgotten Clock-Out

Joe runs a press on the afternoon shift. At the end of the day he heads home without tapping end on his punch. Seeing the punch still open at the close of the shift, the system auto-closes it at midnight, so the record now shows eight hours Joe never worked.

The next morning the shift lead notices the runaway punch. Rather than deleting it and typing a fresh one, the lead opens the punch, adjusts the end time from midnight to five in the afternoon, and enters the reason "operator forgot to clock out." The hours are now right, payroll and job costing get the real number, and the record permanently shows original end midnight, corrected end five, reason recorded, authored by the shift lead. Anyone auditing the day sees the full story, not a suspiciously clean figure.
