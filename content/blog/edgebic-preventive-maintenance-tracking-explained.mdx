**EDGEBIC by User Solutions tracks a machine's service cadence with two fields, an interval in days and the date of the last completed service, and raises a due banner at the shop floor kiosk where the operator will actually see it.** The due date is derived every time rather than stored, so it is always current, and the reminder is deliberately kept separate from anything that blocks capacity.

This is a small feature with a specific job: put the maintenance signal in front of the person about to start a job on the machine, rather than in a report somebody reads later.

## Two Fields, One Derived Date

A work center carries an optional maintenance interval, expressed in days, and an optional date of the last completed service. Thirty for monthly, ninety for quarterly, three hundred and sixty five for annual. Neither field is required, and a machine with no interval set tracks nothing and shows nothing.

When both are present, the due date is the last service date plus the interval, worked out at the moment it is needed rather than written down anywhere. That choice has a practical consequence worth appreciating: there is no stored due date to go stale, no nightly job to recalculate, and no chance of the reminder disagreeing with the fields it came from. Change the interval and every machine's position in its cycle moves with it immediately.

## What the Operator Sees

The signal lands at the [shop floor kiosk](/blog/edgebic-shop-floor-kiosk-explained), on the machine it belongs to, at the moment work is being started there.

- **Overdue.** The derived due date is today or earlier. The banner says service is past due on this machine.
- **Approaching.** The due date falls within the next seven days. The banner gives advance warning while there is still time to plan the window rather than react to it.
- **Nothing.** Either the machine is comfortably inside its cycle, or no interval is configured for it.

The kiosk banner is informational. It does not stop the operator starting a job, and it does not require an acknowledgment before work can proceed. A machine that is one day overdue on a ninety-day cycle is usually fine to run; a machine that is three weeks overdue probably is not, and that judgment belongs to the supervisor standing there, not to a hard block in software. The banner's job is to make sure the judgment gets made at all.

## Why the Reminder Does Not Block Capacity

This is the design decision most worth understanding, because it looks at first like a gap.

A maintenance reminder does not remove hours from the schedule. If the press is overdue, the scheduler will still plan work on it, and the plan will still show its normal capacity. To stop work being booked during the service you enter a [downtime event](/blog/edgebic-downtime-events-explained) or a capacity change on the affected hours, and re-run.

Keeping those two separate buys two things. A due reminder can never silently delete capacity you were counting on, which is what would happen if a cadence field quietly closed a day the moment a counter ticked over. And blocked capacity always reflects a decision a human made about when the service happens, which is the only form the scheduler can honestly plan around. Maintenance being due is a fact; maintenance happening on Thursday afternoon is a decision, and only the decision belongs in the plan.

## The Habit That Keeps It Honest

Everything derives from the last service date, so updating that field when the service is completed is the whole discipline. Skip it and the banner nags forever on a machine that was serviced last week, which trains everyone to ignore banners. Set it forward optimistically and the banner never fires on a machine that is genuinely overdue.

Two supporting habits help. Set the interval from how the machine is actually maintained rather than from the manual's ideal, because a cadence nobody follows produces permanent overdue banners that are indistinguishable from noise. And configure the fields for the machines that matter first, typically the constraint and anything whose failure stops a line, rather than trying to populate every work center at once.

## How It Fits With the Rest of the Plan

Maintenance touches the schedule at three points, and it is useful to see them as a sequence.

| Stage | Mechanism | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Maintenance interval and last service date | Raises the due banner at the kiosk |
| Decision | Downtime event or capacity change | Removes the hours the service will consume |
| Consequence | Re-run the schedule | Moves work off those hours honestly |

The third stage is the one people skip. Blocking the hours without re-running leaves the plan showing jobs on a machine that will be in pieces, and the shift after finds out the hard way. Rescheduling after a capacity change is routine and safe: completed and in-progress work keeps its actual dates, and only future work moves, which is the standing guarantee described in [how EDGEBIC preserves completed work on reschedule](/blog/how-edgebic-preserves-completed-work-on-reschedule).

Quote work respects the same blocks, which is why [a quote scenario respects maintenance downtime](/blog/why-a-quote-scenario-respects-maintenance-downtime-in-edgebic) rather than promising dates that assume a machine nobody will have.

## What It Is Not

It is worth being precise about scope so expectations match the mechanism. This is service-cadence tracking, not a maintenance management system. There is no work-order backlog for the maintenance department, no parts inventory tied to a service, no meter-based or cycle-count triggering, and no condition monitoring feeding it. The cadence is measured in calendar days from the last service.

For plants running a full maintenance program, the value here is the tie to the schedule rather than the tracking itself: the machine's service state shows up where production decisions are being made, and the hours the service consumes go into the same finite capacity model as everything else. That coupling is what a standalone maintenance system usually cannot give you.

## The Point of a Banner at the Machine

Maintenance schedules fail in a predictable way: the cadence is known, the record lives somewhere central, and the person who could act on it is standing at a machine with no view of that record. Putting the due signal on the kiosk closes the distance. It costs two fields on a work center and it means the question "is this machine due" gets asked by the person best placed to answer it, at the moment it matters, instead of being answered by a breakdown three weeks later.
