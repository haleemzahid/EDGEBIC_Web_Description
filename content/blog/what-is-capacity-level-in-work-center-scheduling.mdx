**Capacity level is a banded classification of how heavily a work center is loaded, grouping utilization into low, medium, and high.** Instead of forcing a planner to read a precise percentage on every machine, it turns the load into three at-a-glance signals: barely used, healthy, or near full. The bands make idle capacity and looming overloads visible across a whole plant in a single scan. EDGEBIC by User Solutions uses capacity level so a busy shop can be read like a traffic light rather than a spreadsheet.

## How it works

Capacity level sits on top of utilization, the exact fraction of a work center's available capacity that scheduled work consumes. Utilization is a continuous number; capacity level buckets it into three ranges. Low covers roughly 0 to 50%, medium covers roughly 51 to 80%, and high covers roughly 81 to 100%.

Each band carries a meaning a planner can act on. Low means the machine is barely used and may be a candidate for consolidation or for absorbing extra work. Medium is the healthy operating band, loaded but with room to react to a change. High means the machine is approaching full, where one more job risks an overload, so it deserves a look before you add work.

The value of banding is scale. On a shop with dozens of machines, three signals are far easier to read than dozens of percentages. You scan for the high band to find risk, the low band to find spare capacity, and you leave the medium band alone.

## A concrete example

Think of a highway with variable-message signs. The signs do not display exact vehicle counts; they show green for free-flowing, amber for busy, and red for congested. A driver reads the color instantly and reacts. The precise traffic count still exists underneath, but the band is what makes the road readable at a glance.

Capacity level works the same way for machines. A mill at 30% utilization sits in the low band: plenty of room, maybe underused. A mill at 70% sits in the medium band: healthy, working, still flexible. A mill at 92% sits in the high band: nearly full, one rush job away from an overload. The percentage is still there when you want it; the band tells you where to look first.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

Capacity level drives the color coding on the capacity views, so a planner sees load as bands rather than raw figures. Reading those bands is the subject of [how to read the capacity heatmap in EDGEBIC](/blog/how-to-read-the-capacity-heatmap-in-edgebic), and the specific case of an overloaded day is covered in [reading a red day on the EDGEBIC capacity view](/blog/reading-a-red-day-on-the-edgebic-capacity-view).

Because the bands are built from utilization, they inherit whatever that number is built from: the shifts, holidays, efficiency, and the availability percentage that set a machine's real ceiling. A band that looks wrong usually traces back to one of those inputs, not to the banding itself.

Capacity level is the summary; the precise figure underneath it is the companion term [capacity utilization](/blog/what-is-capacity-utilization-in-scheduling).
