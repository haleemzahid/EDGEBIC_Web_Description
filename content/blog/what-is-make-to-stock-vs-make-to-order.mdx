**Make-to-stock builds product ahead of demand and holds it in inventory ready to ship, while make-to-order builds nothing until a customer order exists and then produces to that specific order.** The two policies sit at opposite ends of the inventory-versus-lead-time trade. Make-to-stock spends inventory to buy fast delivery; make-to-order spends lead time to avoid holding stock. Which one fits depends on whether the item is standard and predictable or custom and variable.

## How it works

The book's analogy captures it in two images. Make-to-stock is a supermarket's bread aisle: loaves are baked each morning and sit on the shelf until a customer picks one up. The customer waits seconds because the work was done ahead of the order. Make-to-order is a custom wedding cake: nothing is baked until the couple orders a specific design, and then it is built to that order alone. The customer waits days because the work starts only after the commitment.

That distinction cascades through the whole plan:

- **Inventory posting.** A make-to-stock order that completes posts a receipt into the ledger, adding finished goods to stock. A make-to-order order posts no receipt; its output ships direct.
- **Demand handling.** Make-to-stock products carry a projected balance, respond to forecast, and can trigger replenishment when stock runs low. Make-to-order products ignore forecast entirely and are driven only by firm orders.
- **Consumption.** When demand arrives for a make-to-stock item with stock on hand, the engine can satisfy it from the shelf instead of building. A make-to-order item is always built to its order.

Because the two behaviors are so different, the policy is a first-class product attribute, and most real shops carry a mix of both across their catalog.

## A concrete example

Widget A is a standard product set to make-to-stock. A build order for 200 completes and posts a +200 receipt to the ledger, so 200 units now sit in finished goods. A customer order for 80 arrives; because stock exists, the engine satisfies it from the shelf and posts an 80-unit issue, leaving 120 on hand. The customer waited only as long as it took to pick and ship, because the build happened ahead of the order.

Now consider a custom fixture set to make-to-order. No stock is held. When a customer orders three, the scheduler builds exactly three against that order, ships them, and posts no receipt to inventory. The next order will trigger another build from scratch, because nothing was made ahead.

A hybrid case ties the two together. A normally make-to-order shop occasionally needs to build a standard sub-part for stock. Rather than create a second part number, the planner overrides that one order to build to inventory, and its output posts a receipt like any make-to-stock build, while every other order for the part stays make-to-order.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

In EDGEBIC, the policy lives on each product as a build method with three settings: make-to-order, make-to-stock, and a hybrid make-to-stock-with-override. The default drives whether the product carries a projected balance and replenishment logic and whether completed builds post receipts. A companion stocked flag gates all inventory activity for the product; a make-to-stock product must be marked stocked or netting is skipped, a mismatch an inventory anomaly check flags.

Individual orders can override the product default through a per-order build-to-inventory setting. Left blank, the order inherits the product policy; set explicitly, it forces make-to-stock or make-to-order for that order alone. The resolution rule is straightforward: the order's flag wins when present, otherwise the product policy applies. This lets one part number serve both a steady make-to-stock stream and the occasional customer-specific build without duplicating master data.
