**A work center type classifies what a resource does, such as Production, Assembly, Quality, Shipping, or Maintenance.** It is the descriptive label on a station that says what kind of work happens there: a lathe is Production, a build line is Assembly, a measurement room is Quality, the dock is Shipping, the tool crib is Maintenance. The type is how a shop groups and reads its resources by function. It answers what a station is for, which is a separate question from how much capacity it has or how its steps behave when they run in parallel.

## How it works

The work center type is a classification, not a calculation. Its everyday values name the function of a station. Production covers standard manufacturing operations like turning, drilling, and welding. Assembly covers combining components into a larger whole. Quality covers inspection and test, such as a coordinate-measuring room. Shipping is the dispatch area. Maintenance covers support functions like a tool crib. These labels let a planner group the shop, filter reports, and understand at a glance what a resource is meant to do.

Alongside those functional values, the same classification carries a Normal value and parallel values that describe how a routing step uses work centers rather than what a station is for. Normal means the step uses one work center with no special parallel behavior. The parallel values mean the step runs on several work centers at the same time, either independently with their own capacity or mirrored in lockstep with a parent. These share the enum but answer a different question, so it helps to keep the two purposes clear in your head.

What the functional type does not do is drive the capacity math. How much time a station offers, how many instances it has, whether it is a bottleneck, whether it runs one job per day, and how its parallel steps behave are all separate settings. The type describes the station; those settings schedule it.

## A concrete example

Walk a small shop floor and label each station. The two lathes and the mill are Production, because they cut metal. The bench where parts come together into a pump is Assembly. The room with the measurement machine, where finished parts are checked against the drawing, is Quality. The loading dock is Shipping. The crib where tooling is maintained is Maintenance.

With those labels in place, a manager can ask clean questions. How loaded is Assembly this week? Which Quality stations are the constraint? Are we shipping on time from the dock? Each is a filter on the type. But when the scheduler decides that the mill can only take one job per day, or that the measurement room has two instances, or that a bottleneck oven should anchor its jobs, those decisions come from the stations' own capacity settings, not from the fact that one is labeled Production and another Quality. The labels organize the shop; the settings run it.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

In EDGEBIC, the work center type is master data that classifies each resource by function, so the shop can be grouped, filtered, and reported by what its stations actually do. Production, Assembly, Quality, Shipping, and Maintenance give a plant a shared vocabulary for its resources, which keeps dashboards and reports readable as the shop grows.

The scheduling engine plans against a station's concrete capacity rather than its functional label. The hours a work center offers come from its shifts, instances, availability percentage, and any capacity overrides; whether it constrains the plant comes from its bottleneck flag; how its steps overlap comes from the routing. So the functional type is descriptive, giving structure to how you view the plant, while those operational settings determine when and how work lands.

The Normal and parallel values in the same classification sit closer to scheduling, but they describe the routing step's behavior, single work center versus several at once, rather than the station's function. Keeping the descriptive types and the step-behavior values conceptually separate is what makes the classification easy to reason about.
