**EDGEBIC by User Solutions includes a work center schedule view that filters the whole plan down to a single station, showing only the operations booked to that machine in planned-start order, so a supervisor sees exactly what to run and in what sequence without the noise of every other resource.** A plant-wide schedule is the right tool for a planner and the wrong tool for the person running one machine. The station view exists for that person: it trades breadth for focus and hands one work center its own queue.

This is the tightest zoom in the family of schedule surfaces. The [timeline board](/blog/edgebic-timeline-board-explained) shows every resource at once for cross-plant balancing; the station view drops to one resource and goes deep. Both read the same committed schedule, so the sequence you see here is the sequence the board shows in that lane.

## One Station, Its Own Queue

The core idea is subtraction. Pick a work center, and everything that is not booked to it disappears. What remains is that station's operations, ordered the way the scheduler intends them to run, which is by planned start. Top to bottom is the run sequence.

That subtraction is more valuable than it sounds. At shift start, a supervisor reading a full plant schedule has to hunt for their station's rows among hundreds of others, and skimming past a row that was actually theirs is an easy, expensive mistake. The station view removes the hunt: the list is already only theirs, already in order. Read it down, confirm the first operations are ready to go, and start.

## Remaining Work, Not the Original Estimate

A station view that only showed the original plan would mislead the moment a job is partly run. EDGEBIC reads the live schedule together with the logged [actuals](/blog/edgebic-actuals-tracking-explained), so a half-finished operation reflects what is genuinely left, not the estimate it started with.

This matters most for the decision an operator makes constantly: does this fit before the shift ends. An operation that started as a ten-hour job but has six hours logged has four hours left, and four hours is the number that decides whether to start it now or line up something shorter. Showing remaining work rather than the original figure is the difference between a queue you can trust and one you have to mentally correct.

## Focus Versus Breadth

It helps to place the station view against its neighbors, because they all read the same schedule and each is right for a different person and moment.

A planner refereeing contention wants the [timeline board](/blog/edgebic-timeline-board-explained) or the [resource load view](/blog/edgebic-resource-load-view-explained), which show many resources so you can compare and balance. A supervisor running one station wants this view, which shows one resource in depth. And when the sequence needs to leave the screen and go to the floor as paper, the [dispatch report](/blog/how-to-run-filter-and-export-reports-in-edgebic) is the point-in-time snapshot you filter to a station and export. Focus, breadth, and paper: three tools, one schedule.

## Live Through the Shift

Because the station view reads the live plan, it is not a morning-only artifact. As the shift progresses and actuals are logged, the remaining-work numbers update and the queue reflects reality. A supervisor can glance at it midday to see whether the station is tracking to plan or falling behind, and act before the shortfall compounds.

If the station is falling behind and the cause is a genuine overload rather than a slow run, the [load view](/blog/reading-a-red-day-on-the-edgebic-capacity-view) confirms whether the machine is booked past its capacity, and the reschedule tools move work if it is. The station view surfaces the symptom; the capacity and reschedule surfaces diagnose and treat it.

## Where It Fits

In the daily loop, the station view is the last mile. Planners set and balance the schedule using the plant-wide surfaces; the scheduler solves the timing; and each station reads its own queue here to execute. It is deliberately the least glamorous of the schedule views because its job is not analysis, it is handing the right work, in the right order, to the person standing at the machine.

For the whole scheduling system these views sit inside, the [complete EDGEBIC guide](/blog/edgebic-complete-guide) is the map, and the [dashboard tabs](/blog/how-to-use-the-edgebic-dashboard-tabs) give the plant-wide read that tells a planner which station to open first.

## The Point of Zooming to One Machine

A schedule only creates value when the work reaches the machine in the right order, and the person at the machine should not have to earn that order by hunting through a plant-wide plan. Filtering to one station, in run sequence, with honest remaining-work numbers, is what turns a scheduling result into a shift a supervisor can simply run. It is the plan, delivered to the point where it is executed.
