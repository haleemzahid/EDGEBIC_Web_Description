**A finite capacity scheduler handles a shared tool or fixture most reliably when you model the tool as its own work center that the routing passes through, giving it as many instances as you own copies of the tool.** EDGEBIC by User Solutions does not attach a dedicated tooling-resource type to every operation automatically, so the honest, shipped way to make a fixture, die, or mold a real constraint is to turn it into a work center. Because a work center is a genuine capacity bucket, the finite capacity engine then prevents the plan from booking more jobs on the tool than you have copies of it. The contention becomes a visible start-time shift instead of a floor collision.

## Why a shared tool needs modeling at all

A shared fixture, die, or mold is a resource just like a machine, but shops rarely track it as one. The machine schedule looks fine, the mills sit at 60 percent, and yet dates slip. The reason is that two jobs on two different machines both need the same fixture, and only one fixture exists. On paper both jobs are feasible; on the floor one of them waits.

That wait never appears in a machine utilization report, because no machine is overloaded. It shows up only as unexplained work in process and missed dates. A plan that does not know about the shared tool will happily promise both jobs at once and be wrong the moment the floor reaches for the second fixture. The generic problem, and how to spot it, is covered in [when tooling is your bottleneck](/blog/tooling-bottleneck-scheduling).

## What EDGEBIC does and does not do

Be clear about the honest state. EDGEBIC has a physical-type label for a resource that can read "tool," but that label carries no scheduling logic. There is no automatic secondary-resource layer that pegs a specific die to a routing step and checks its calendar behind the scenes. Dedicated shared-tooling as a distinct resource class is a roadmap item, not shipped behavior.

What is shipped, and what you should use, is the finite capacity engine itself. A work center is already a resource that the engine will never over-book: it has instances, shifts, and capacity, and an operation that routes through it consumes real hours. So you make the tool a work center. That is not a hack around a missing feature; it is using the constraint model you already have to represent a constraint the engine treats as first class.

## Model the tool as a work center the routing passes through

The pattern is direct. Create a work center that stands for the shared tool, for example "Fixture Set A." Set its instance count to the number of physical copies you own: two fixtures means two instances. Then add that work center to the routing of every operation that requires the tool, so the step passes through both its machine and the tool work center.

With the tool as a two-instance work center, the plan allows at most two jobs to hold the fixture at any moment. A third fixture job cannot schedule until one of the two frees up, no matter how many mills are idle. The ceiling you feel on the floor is now the ceiling the plan enforces.

Two settings sharpen the model. If the tool locks for a whole day once it is set, such as a mold with a long changeover, set the tool work center to one job per instance per day, so the plan will not try to share a single copy across two jobs in the same day. This one-per-day behavior matches a paint booth or a press mold, where a color or die change ties up the resource for the shift. And put the changeover hours on the routing step's setup time so the plan accounts for the swap. Work centers, instances, and the one-per-day flag are covered in [how a scheduler picks an instance](/blog/how-edgebic-picks-an-instance-load-balancing-vs-one-per-day).

## A worked example

A shop has three mills, Mill-1, Mill-2, Mill-3, each free most of the week. It owns two copies of Fixture Set A. Four jobs this week all need Fixture Set A, each four hours, all releasable Monday.

Model Fixture Set A as a work center with two instances. Route all four jobs through their mill plus Fixture Set A.

Monday, the plan places Job 1 on Mill-1 and Job 2 on Mill-2, both holding a fixture, 08:00 to 12:00. Job 3 wants Mill-3 at 08:00, but both fixture instances are taken until 12:00, so the forward search moves Job 3 to 12:00, when a fixture frees. Job 4 follows at 12:00 on the second freed fixture.

The mills show plenty of idle time, yet the plan spreads the four jobs across the day because the fixture, not the mill, is the binding resource. That is the true picture. Without the fixture work center, the plan would stack all four Monday morning and the floor would have two jobs waiting for a fixture by 08:05.

## What this buys you, and its limits

The payoff is a schedule that reflects the real ceiling. You can now see whether a third fixture would pay off, sequence jobs to reduce fixture contention, and quote lead times that hold. Blocking planned tool maintenance is a matter of giving the tool work center a capacity override or holiday on the maintenance day, so operations route around it automatically.

The limit is honesty about the model. The tool work center does not track a mold's physical location or its shot-count maintenance trigger on its own, and it will not automatically follow a specific fixture between machines beyond the capacity cap you set. For most shops the cap is the constraint that matters, and modeling it as a work center captures it cleanly. If you need richer tool tracking, treat this pattern as the reliable base and plan the rest as a manual master-data discipline.

The broader idea is the same one behind every finite constraint: name the scarce resource, give it real capacity, and let the engine refuse to over-book it.
