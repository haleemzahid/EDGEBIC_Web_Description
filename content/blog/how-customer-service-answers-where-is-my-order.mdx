The customer service rep answers while the customer is still on the phone.

## What a Status Call Costs Today

The customer asks a simple question. Here is the path it takes in a plant without a progress record:

1. The rep does not know, and says they will check.
2. The rep calls or walks to the supervisor.
3. The supervisor does not know precisely and goes to look.
4. An operator is interrupted mid-setup to confirm.
5. The supervisor reports back.
6. The rep calls the customer a second time.

Six steps, three people, and two customer contacts for one question. Multiply by twenty inquiries a week and the floor time alone is around three and a half hours, arriving in fragments at exactly the moments interruptions are most expensive.

The second contact is the part customers notice. "I will find out and call you back" reads as a shop that does not know what it is doing, even when the answer, once found, is perfectly good news.

## Three Reports, Three Grains

The status question comes in three sizes, and each has its own report.

| The question | Report | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| How is my whole order doing? | Sales Order Progress | The customer's order rolled up |
| How far along is that job? | Job Progress | Percent complete, one row per job, no date range |
| Which step is behind? | Work Center Progress | Step-grain detail under the job |

Job Progress is the workhorse and it carries one rule worth knowing before you quote it to a customer: a job reads Completed only when every operation step has an actual end date. Marking one early step complete does not flip the job, and the finish date stays empty until the last step is done.

Percent complete is hours-first, actual hours divided by planned hours, falling back to pieces only when no hours exist. That keeps the number honest. A job showing 92 percent with one operation untouched is genuinely not finished, and a system that rounded it to done would be setting up a conversation nobody wants to have on the ship date.

## From "Where Is It" to "Why, and When"

The better version of the call answers the follow-up before it is asked. Four lookups get you there:

**Late Jobs** shows how late a job is, ranked, with a blocker hint on the row.

**Work Center Progress**, filtered to that job, names the operation that stalled and shows hours logged against hours planned on it.

**Reschedule History** shows whether the job was moved, by how much, and on which run.

**Work Center Utilization**, with its backlog drill-down, shows whether the station holding it is overloaded and which jobs are ahead of it.

Together those produce a sentence you can actually say: the mill operation has 41 of 101 hours logged with nothing added in three days, the job was pushed four days on Thursday's run, and the mill is running above 100 percent with two rush jobs inserted ahead of it. That is a cause and a plan. It replaces an apology, and it takes about ten minutes rather than two days. ## The Arithmetic

For a plant handling twenty status inquiries a week:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Floor interruptions per inquiry | 2 | 0 |
| Minutes of floor time per inquiry | ~10 | 0 |
| Floor time per week | ~200 minutes | 0 |
| Customer contacts per inquiry | 2 | 1 |
| Time to answer | hours | ~30 seconds |

Three and a half hours a week of supervisor and operator time is the visible saving, and it is real. The larger one is the halving of customer contacts, because the second call is where a routine question turns into an anxious one. A customer who gets an immediate, specific answer usually stops calling; a customer who gets a callback tomorrow starts calling weekly.

## Who Should Be Reading It

The change that makes this stick is not the report, it is who has access to it. If only the planner can open the progress reports, every status question still routes through one person and you have moved the bottleneck rather than removed it.

Give customer service read access to the progress family. They will use it because it is faster than asking, and the planner stops being an information desk. That is the same principle behind putting the standup board on a screen instead of reading status aloud in a meeting. ## What the Reports Cannot Tell a Customer

**They are only as current as the logging.** Percent complete reads logged hours. If operators log at the end of the week, Thursday's answer describes last Friday, and a confident wrong answer is worse than a callback. See [EDGEBIC actuals tracking explained](/blog/edgebic-actuals-tracking-explained).

**They are point-in-time.** Every report queries fresh when you open it and caches nothing, which means it is accurate at the moment you read it and says nothing about the next hour. When a report drives a commitment, export it so the promise keeps its arithmetic.

**Percent complete is not percent of the way to the door.** It measures hours against the plan for the operations on the job. Packing, inspection outside the routing, and shipping lead time are separate, and a customer hearing 95 percent will assume otherwise unless you say so.

**A forecast late is not a promise broken.** On-Time Delivery judges finished jobs by their actual end and unfinished jobs by their scheduled end, which makes it an early-warning list as well as a scorecard. Telling a customer a job is trending late while there is still time to act is a different call from telling them it missed. See the generic [on-time delivery KPI](/blog/on-time-delivery-kpi).

**It cannot see work run outside the plan.** A job pulled forward on the floor and never entered will read as untouched. The record is complete for what the software planned and blind to what it never saw. See [what production scheduling is](/blog/what-is-production-scheduling) for the underlying model.
