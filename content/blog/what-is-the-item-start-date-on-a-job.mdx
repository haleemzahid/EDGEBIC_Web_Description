**The Item Start date on a manufacturing order is the moment its last work-center operation ends, which is when the manufactured item physically exists. In EDGEBIC by User Solutions it excludes material rows and counts only real operations, and despite its name it marks a beginning rather than an end: production stops there and the end-item lead-time clock starts, so Item Start plus the product's lead time gives the delivery-ready Job End date that lateness is actually measured against.**

This entry is part of the EDGEBIC glossary series; for the broader vocabulary of production planning, see the [manufacturing glossary](/blog/manufacturing-glossary).

## How Item Start Works

Every scheduled job produces a set of operation-level dates, one pair per routing step. Item Start is a job-level roll-up of those: it is the latest end date among the job's work-center operations.

Two details in that sentence carry weight.

**It is the latest end, not the last step in sequence.** On a routing with independent parallel branches, or with a sub-assembly feeding a final operation, the step that finishes last is not always the step drawn last. The engine takes the maximum, so a slow parallel branch that overruns the main line correctly pushes Item Start out.

**Material rows do not count.** A routing may carry material lines that consume no work-center capacity. Those are excluded from the calculation, because a material issue does not make an item exist. Only operations on real work centers move the Item Start date.

What Item Start is not is a delivery date. Between the last machine and the loading dock sits whatever the product needs: a paint cure, a pack-out, an inspection hold, a freight leg. That time is carried on the product record as end-item lead time in calendar days, and it consumes no work-center capacity. Add it to Item Start and you have [Job End](/blog/what-is-the-job-end-date-on-a-job), the delivery-ready date.

## A Worked Example

From the documentation's sample plant: Widget-A carries a **Lead Time** of 2 days. JOB-2026-0101's last operation, on **Assembly-1**, finishes Thursday at 14:00.

Item Start is Thursday 14:00. Job End is Saturday 14:00. If the due date is Friday, the job is late, even though the machines finished on Thursday and every operator involved would tell you the job was done ahead of time.

That is the whole lesson of the pair in one example. Production met its date; delivery did not. The two days of packing pushed delivery-readiness past the promise, and a plant reading only the shop-floor number would have found out at the loading dock.

The same sample world shows the opposite case on JOB-2026-0102: Item Start lands Tuesday July 21 at 10:00, the product carries one day of lead time, Job End is Wednesday July 22, and the due date is also Wednesday July 22, so Days Late reads 0. Here the tail fits and nobody has to think about it. Set Lead Time to 0 on any product and Item Start and Job End become identical, which is exactly why plants with no post-production tail can go years without noticing the distinction exists.

## How EDGEBIC Uses Item Start

Item Start appears as its own column on the Drive Schedule grid and again in the job-level trio on the Job View grid, sitting beside Lead Time and Job End so the arithmetic is visible rather than implied. It is used deliberately in a few places and deliberately not used in others:

- **It is the shop-floor completion date.** When a supervisor asks when a job comes off the last machine, Item Start is the answer. It is the moment the part is available to pack, inspect, or move to finished goods.
- **It starts the lead-time clock.** The end-item lead time is applied forward in calendar days from Item Start, not from a shift boundary, because cure and freight time does not observe your shift pattern.
- **It is not what lateness is judged against.** Days Late, the dashboard job status buckets, and the Gantt's late coloring all classify against Job End. Every surface agrees on that, which is why a job can show a green Item Start and a red status.
- **It responds to the plan, not to product edits.** Item Start only changes when the operations themselves move, which means a schedule run. Editing a product's lead time changes Job End immediately and leaves Item Start exactly where it was.
- **Backward scheduling reserves the tail up front.** A backward-scheduled job is built so the lead-time tail lands inside the promise, which means the engine targets an Item Start early enough for the tail to fit rather than aiming the last operation at the due date. See [end-item lead time in scheduling](/blog/what-is-end-item-lead-time-in-scheduling).

The practical habit is to treat Item Start as the internal number and Job End as the external one. Read Item Start when you are talking to the floor and Job End when you are talking to a customer, and the two dates stop being confusing and start being useful. The [backward scheduling explainer](/blog/edgebic-backward-scheduling-explained) shows how the tail is placed, and [days late on a job](/blog/what-is-days-late-on-a-job) covers what happens when the pair does not fit inside the promise.
