**CNC operator skill scheduling models the certified operator as a finite resource alongside the machine, so EDGEBIC by User Solutions plans on the hours your setters and programmers actually have, not just the number of spindles you own.** For a CNC shop, the constraint is rarely the machine. It is the one person who can prove a five-axis program, run the CMM, or set up the Swiss lathe. This post is about scheduling that person into the plan, and what it does to lights-out running in particular.

The mechanics of building a realistic finite plan are in [what is production scheduling](/blog/what-is-production-scheduling). The idea that the true limit is often a single resource is the theme of [production bottleneck identification](/blog/production-bottleneck-identification). Here the bottleneck wears steel-toe boots.

## The schedule that only sees machines

Until you tell it otherwise, a scheduler sees a work center as capacity: shifts, holidays, machine count. It has no concept of the person who has to stand at that machine. So a shop with one operator certified on Inconel can happily schedule five Inconel jobs on five machines in the same shift, and the plan is fiction before the first tool touches metal.

CNC shops feel this sharply because the skill gap is real and narrow. The programmer who can prove the tricky part, the setter trusted on the multi-axis, the inspector on the CMM. When the schedule assumes any machine can start any job at any time, it overcommits those people daily, and the floor spends the morning re-juggling what the plan got wrong overnight. This is the "silent slip" that makes crews stop trusting the system and go back to a printed list.

## Skills, certifications, and an hours ledger

The fix is to make the operator a second constraint on the routing step. A step names the skill it needs, for example a five-axis setup or a CMM inspection. Operators hold certifications for the skills they are qualified on, with optional expiry dates so a lapsed cert drops out of the plan automatically. A weekly roster records which shifts each operator works, and time-off ranges record their absences.

The engine turns that into a per-operator ledger of hours, keyed by operator, date, and shift. When it schedules a step that needs a skill, it asks a simple question at each candidate window: does a qualified operator, rostered for this shift and not on leave, still have hours left that day? A window with no staffed hours is never offered; the step slides forward to the next day someone qualified is available. And the hours it commits are capped at what the qualified pool can afford, so a day can never oversell a person.

The result is the everyday sanity check the machine-only plan lacked. One welder certified on Inconel, two Inconel jobs on two machines: the first job books the welder Monday, the second finds Monday's pool empty and moves to Tuesday. Certify a second person and both run Monday, one on each machine, exactly as a foreman would staff it.

## Lights-out machining: the setter is the constraint, not the run

Here is where CNC shops win. Most machining does not need a person for the whole cut. It needs a person for the setup, then the machine runs itself. Operator skill scheduling models that directly with an attention figure on the run phase.

Set the run attention to zero, meaning setup-only, and the step gates the operator on setup alone. The run books nobody, so cutting can execute on completely unstaffed evening hours. Setup always books one full operator, because a person sets a machine up.

Take seven CNC mills, one certified operator with eight hours a day, and seven jobs that each need one hour of setup and nine hours of unattended run.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Operator cost per job | 1 hour setup, 0 for the run |
| Operator's daily hours | 8 |
| Jobs that can start on day one | 7 (seven hours of setup) |
| The eighth job | Fits in the last hour |
| The ninth setup | Slides to the next day |

Seven machines start Monday, the operator spends seven of eight hours setting up, and the cutting runs itself into the evening. The ninth setup does not fit and moves to Tuesday, honestly. This is a lights-out plan that is actually staffable, which a machine-count plan can never produce. The attention concept is explained in full in [what attend fraction means in operator scheduling](/blog/what-is-attend-fraction-operator-scheduling).

The same knob covers the other cases. Set it to 0.5 and one operator tends two machines: eight of their hours buy sixteen machine-hours of attended running. Set it to 1.0 for fully attended work, one operator-hour per machine-hour. Set it to 2.0 for a two-person job, where each machine-hour books two operator-hours.

## Pinning the job to a named person

Sometimes the work must run on one specific person's hands, not just anyone with the certificate. Pin the step to that operator and the plan staffs it only with them. The step then slides through their vacation and roster gaps rather than silently handing the work to someone else. A named person's absence becomes visible schedule movement, which is exactly what you want a planner to see and approve, not a substitution nobody signed off on. Pin a job to a person who is not certified for its skill and the plan fails fast with a clear reason, before it schedules anything wrong.

## Where this sits next to setup sequencing and alternates

Operator scheduling composes cleanly with the other CNC levers. Sequence-dependent setup times, covered in [CNC setup sequencing](/blog/edgebic-for-cnc-shops-setup-sequencing), decide how long a changeover takes; the operator ledger decides whether a qualified person is there to do it. Alternate machines, covered in [alternate machine scheduling](/blog/edgebic-for-cnc-shops-alternate-machines), give a job more than one place to run; the skill check narrows that to the machines a staffed operator can actually cover. And the whole thing sits on top of the shift and machine model behind [lights-out CNC scheduling](/blog/edgebic-for-cnc-shops-lights-out-scheduling), so unattended running and staffed running share one plan.

When no operator data is loaded, or on steps with no skill, every one of these checks does nothing and the plan behaves exactly as a machine-only plan would. You add the constraint where it matters, on the certified work, and leave everything else alone.

## Setting it up in a CNC shop

Three steps get you to a staffable plan. Create the skills your work actually gates on, the specific certifications that separate your senior people from the rest. Certify and roster those operators, with expiry dates where certs lapse and time-off ranges for their leave. Then decide the attention on each skill-bearing step: zero for lights-out setup-only running, a fraction for one person on several machines, one for fully attended, more for crews.

From there the plan tells the truth about labor. The five-axis cell starts only as many machines as a setter can prove, the senior programmer's queue shows up as schedule movement instead of a pile-up on the floor, and vacations move work rather than breaking promises.
