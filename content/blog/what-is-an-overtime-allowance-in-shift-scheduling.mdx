**An overtime allowance is a shift's declared overtime policy, a flag for whether overtime is permitted plus a maximum hours per day that acts as a ceiling for overtime calculations, and in EDGEBIC it documents intent rather than silently stretching the base plan.** It is where a shift states its boundary in one clear place.

This entry defines the overtime allowance and shows how it reads inside [EDGEBIC by User Solutions](/edgebic). For the wider index of terms, see the [manufacturing glossary](/blog/manufacturing-glossary); for the base hours it sits on top of, see [what is shift time in production scheduling](/blog/what-is-shift-time-in-production-scheduling); and for the full calendar setup, see [EDGEBIC shifts and calendars explained](/blog/edgebic-shifts-and-calendars-explained).

## How it works

A shift carries two settings that together form its overtime allowance. The first is a flag for whether overtime is allowed on that shift. The second is a maximum hours per day, a ceiling used for overtime calculations, which defaults to eight.

These describe policy. The flag records that the shift may run overtime, and the ceiling records how far a day is meant to stretch when overtime is in play. Read together, they answer "what is this shift allowed to do?" without anyone having to inspect individual schedules.

The important nuance is that the allowance is informational: the base capacity the engine schedules against is the shift's ordinary working hours, decoded from its shift times. Turning on the flag does not automatically add hours to the plan. If you need more capacity on given days, you extend the working hours themselves; the allowance keeps the intent on record alongside them.

## A concrete example

Think of a job posting that says "up to 10 hours per day, overtime available." That line sets expectations, but it does not by itself put anyone on the clock; the actual roster does. An overtime allowance is that job-posting line for a shift: it states the permitted ceiling and that overtime is on the table, while the roster, the shift's working hours, is what determines the hours actually planned.

Take a day shift with the overtime-allowed flag on and a maximum of ten hours per day, but working hours set to eight. The allowance says the shift may go to ten, yet the engine plans eight, because that is what the working hours declare. To actually schedule ten-hour days during a rush, you widen the shift's start and end for those days. The allowance documented the policy; the working hours delivered the capacity.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

In EDGEBIC, the overtime allowance lives on the shift as the overtime-allowed flag and the maximum-hours-per-day ceiling. It gives planners and supervisors a single place to see a shift's overtime policy, which keeps decisions consistent instead of remembered case by case.

Because the base schedule is built from the shift's working hours, the allowance is a statement of intent rather than a lever that quietly extends the plan. That separation is deliberate and honest: the policy is visible, and the hours the engine commits are exactly the working hours you configured, no more. When a rush genuinely needs more capacity, you change the working hours, and the allowance sits beside them recording that overtime is sanctioned and how far it may go.
