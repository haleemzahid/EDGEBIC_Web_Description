**Fire protection equipment is not finished when it is assembled, it is finished when it passes test.** EDGEBIC by User Solutions models test stands as finite capacity, freezes the routing standard each job was built to, and schedules certified operations only where a qualified operator has hours available.

Machining, forming, and assembly in this sector are ordinary. What is not ordinary is that everything you build passes through a small number of test positions, and everything you ship must be provable years later.

## The Test Stand Is the Hidden Constraint

Every valve, every sprinkler head assembly, every suppression cylinder, and every panel goes through some form of test before it leaves. A shop with two hydrostatic stands and thirty fabrication cells is almost always constrained by the two stands, and almost never schedules them.

Model each stand as a work center with real capacity and a calendar. Now a test cannot be promised into a window that is already committed, and stand occupancy becomes something you can read a week ahead instead of something you discover on the test floor. The general principle is in [finite vs infinite capacity scheduling](/blog/finite-vs-infinite-capacity-scheduling).

Then flag the test line as your bottleneck so the schedule anchors around it. Fabrication is released to feed test at the rate test can absorb, rather than at the rate fabrication can produce. That release-rate mismatch, not a shortage of stands, is usually the real cause of the pile of untested assemblies. See [production bottleneck identification](/blog/production-bottleneck-identification).

If your two stands are genuinely interchangeable, pool them into a work center group so a unit goes to whichever frees up first. Details are in [how a work center group shops a pool of machines](/blog/how-a-work-center-group-shops-a-pool-of-machines).

## Proving the Build Standard

Fire protection products are built to listed standards, and the standards get revised. When a revision lands, you always have units in process that were released under the previous one.

When a job is scheduled, the routing in force is captured as a frozen snapshot attached to that job. Revising the master routing today does not reshape a job released last month, so units in process finish to the standard they started under and new releases pick up the revision. Each job can prove which one applied, because the snapshot travels with it through every reschedule. See [how a frozen routing snapshot protects a running job](/blog/how-a-frozen-routing-snapshot-protects-a-running-job).

Alongside the snapshot, every schedule change is recorded: what moved, when, and why. If a date shifted because a standard was revised rather than because the shop was late, the change log says so, and that distinction is usually what the argument is about. See [the schedule change log audit trail](/blog/the-edgebic-schedule-change-log-audit-trail).

## Certified People Are Capacity

Pressure testing, brazing, and final inspection in this sector are performed by qualified people under specific procedures. Six assemblers on the payroll does not mean six people can sign off a test.

Record each operator's certifications and their available hours per shift, then require the certification on the routing steps that need it. The scheduler places those steps only where a qualified operator has hours, so a shift with no qualified inspector cannot receive final inspection work. The job moves to the next shift where the capability exists and the resulting date shift appears on the plan. A neighboring treatment of the same pattern is in [medical devices and operator certifications](/blog/edgebic-for-medical-devices-operator-certifications).

When a certification lapses or an operator leaves, the load that depended on it becomes visible immediately as work with no home, rather than as a Thursday morning surprise.

## Cure, Coat, and Wait Steps

Powder coat cure, adhesive set, and paint dry are elapsed time, not worked time. A four-hour cure started at 2 PM is done at 6 PM whether or not the shift ends at 4.

Put those on the routing as elapsed steps so the calendar carries them straight through, and the next operation is timed off when parts are genuinely ready. If cure happens in an oven with limited racks, model the oven as its own work center with capacity so two lots cannot be promised the same window. A neighboring treatment is [powder coating color changeovers](/blog/edgebic-for-powder-coating-color-changeovers).

## Outside Plating and Certification Testing

Some steps leave the building: plating, specialized nondestructive testing, or third-party witness testing. Put each on the routing with its real transit and process window so the calendar carries the full out-and-back time. A five-day process with a day each way of freight is seven days between the steps around it, and downstream work is scheduled off the actual return rather than off the day the previous in-house step ended.

## A Worked Job

A 300-unit valve assembly order through a shop with two test stands.

| Step | Resource | Time | Placed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine body | Machining pool member picked at schedule time | 2 days | Mon to Tue |
| Sub-assemble | Assembly cell | 1 day | Wed |
| Certified braze | Braze cell, qualified operator required | 1 day | Thu |
| Outside plate | Vendor, 3 days plus 2 transit | 5 days | Fri to next Wed |
| Final assemble | Assembly cell | 1 day | Thu |
| Hydrostatic test | Test stand group member picked at schedule time | 0.5 day | Fri AM |
| Inspect and label | Qualified inspector required | 0.5 day | Fri PM |
| Pack and ship | Dock | 0.5 day | Mon AM |

The braze landed in the shift where a qualified operator had hours, not simply in the next open cell. The plating window is on the calendar, so the second Thursday is real. Test landed on Stand 2 because Stand 1 was committed. Final inspection went where a qualified inspector was actually available.

## Tracking What Really Happened

Operators log start, stop, and quantity at a floor station, so a test that ran long or an assembly that took an extra shift pushes only the work that has not started. Completed operations are never moved by a reschedule, so the record of what actually ran stays intact through every replan. In a sector where the record is part of the product, that immutability matters as much as the plan does.

## Promising a Date on a Project Order

Fire protection work is often quoted against a building schedule where a late delivery holds up an occupancy inspection. Quote simulation runs the proposed order against current finite capacity, including committed test stand time, and returns a realistic window with the load behind it. If it is too late, you test a second test shift or an alternate fabrication route in the same simulation before committing.
