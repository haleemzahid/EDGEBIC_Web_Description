**A department status is the lifecycle state of a department, the organizational grouping that holds a set of work centers, and it takes one of three values: active, inactive, or maintenance.** It describes the standing of a plant area rather than the hour-by-hour availability of the machines inside it. EDGEBIC by User Solutions carries the status on every department so that organizational reality and machine capacity stay two separate, honest statements.

## How it works

A department groups work centers that belong together, such as machining, assembly, or heat treat, and it can also stand for a plant location with its own address. Departments are how a plant rolls capacity and cost up above the individual machine, so a manager can ask about an area rather than about twelve separate work centers.

Because departments outlive individual machines, they need a lifecycle. **Active** is the normal state: the area is operating and its work centers are in service. **Inactive** means the area has been shut down or mothballed. The decommissioned paint shop still exists in the record because history points at it, but nobody expects it back. **Maintenance** is the temporary version: the area is offline for major work, such as a bay being re-roofed or a line being rebuilt, and is expected to return.

The distinction that matters most is between status and capacity. The status is a label on the grouping. It does not create or remove schedulable hours, because hours belong to work centers, not to the folder they sit in. A work center's availability comes from its shifts, the plant and shift holidays that apply to it, its own downtime events, and any capacity overrides a planner has entered.

That separation is intentional. If status quietly removed capacity, then an organizational tidying-up would silently change the production plan, and the two would drift apart with no obvious cause. Keeping them separate means an area that is genuinely closed has to be expressed twice: once as capacity, so the schedule is correct, and once as status, so anyone reading the plan understands why.

The practical rule is to use both together. Set the work centers' availability to reflect reality, and set the department status to explain it.

## A concrete example

Think of a restaurant with a kitchen and a pastry section. The pastry section is a department: it has its own ovens, its own staff, and its own share of the week's output. If the pastry chef is on vacation for a week, the section is still active, it simply has fewer hours available, and that shows up on the roster rather than on the sign.

If the section is closed for a month while the ovens are replaced, the sign on the door says closed for refurbishment. That is maintenance status: a temporary, explained closure, and everyone reading the roster understands why there are no pastry hours in it. If the restaurant drops desserts from the menu permanently and clears the room out, the section becomes inactive. The old orders that mention pastry still make sense, because the section still exists in the records, but nothing new goes there.

Note that in all three cases the roster is what actually decides whether anybody works. The sign explains the roster; it does not replace it.
