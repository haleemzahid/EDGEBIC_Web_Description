**An operator certification in scheduling is the record that links one operator to one skill, carrying the date certified, an optional expiry date, and a proficiency level, so the scheduler knows exactly who is currently qualified to run a step that requires that skill.** It is the bridge between a person and a capability: the skill says what is required, the certification says who holds it, and only active, unexpired certifications count when the engine decides who can be staffed.

This entry is part of the [EDGEBIC by User Solutions](/edgebic) glossary series; for the wider vocabulary of planning and scheduling, see the [manufacturing glossary](/blog/manufacturing-glossary). A certification is only meaningful once a routing step declares a required skill, so read it alongside [what is a skill in manufacturing scheduling](/blog/what-is-a-skill-in-manufacturing-scheduling).

## How an Operator Certification Works

A certification is a small but load-bearing record. It names an operator, names a skill, and stamps three facts: when the certification took effect, when it expires (optional), and how proficient the operator is. On its own it is just data, but the scheduling engine reads it every run to build the pool of people who may work each skilled step.

When the engine assembles its labor feed, it walks the certification links and keeps only the ones that are active and not past their expiry date. That filtered set becomes the qualified pool. A routing step that requires a skill can then only be placed in a window where at least one operator in that pool is rostered, not on leave, and still has unbooked hours.

Because the filter runs fresh on every scheduling pass, expiry is self-enforcing. Nobody has to remember to remove a lapsed operator from a work center. The moment the expiry date passes, the next run simply stops seeing that certification, and the operator quietly leaves the qualified pool for that skill while keeping every other certification intact.

## A Concrete Example

Take Joe, the plant's TIG welder, who holds a TIG-Inconel certification that expires on December 31. Throughout the year, that certification puts Joe in the qualified pool for every Inconel welding step, and the engine happily staffs those steps on him when he has free hours.

On January 1, nothing changes in the routing and nobody edits a work center, yet the schedule behaves differently. The next scheduling run builds the labor feed, sees that Joe's Inconel certification is now past its expiry, and drops it. Joe is still fully certified for mild steel, so his ordinary welding work continues exactly as before, but the Inconel steps no longer land on him. If he was the only Inconel-certified welder, those steps slide out to a later window and the recertification gap becomes visible on the plan.

That is the whole mechanism: a single expiry date on a single link, quietly reshaping who is eligible for what, without a planner touching the schedule by hand.

## How EDGEBIC Uses It

The certification link sits between the skill catalog and the operator roster, and several product behaviors depend on it.

- **The engine's labor feed** is built from active certifications only. Expired or inactive links are filtered out before the qualified pool is formed, so lapsed certifications never staff a step.
- **The qualified pool** for a required skill is the set of operators whose certification is current. A step waits when that pool is exhausted for a window, which is why a plant rich in machine hours can still be short on the right people.
- **Proficiency and dates** live on the link itself, so the same record answers "who is qualified," "since when," and "until when" without a second table.

A certification pairs naturally with an operator's [attend fraction](/blog/what-is-attend-fraction-operator-scheduling), which controls how much of a qualified person's time a running step actually consumes once they are assigned. When you need to lock a step to one named person rather than the whole qualified pool, that is an [operator pin](/blog/what-is-an-operator-pin-in-scheduling), a separate and stronger control that still relies on the underlying certification.
