**Medical device operator hours scheduling plans on a per-operator ledger of available hours, not just a yes-or-no certification check, so EDGEBIC by User Solutions books only the certified-operator time you actually have and slides work when it runs out.** For a medical device manufacturer, the certification question is necessary but not sufficient. Knowing an inspector is qualified does not stop the plan from assuming she can be at five benches at once. This post is about the hours behind the qualification, and how they keep the schedule staffable.

The base finite-capacity model is [what is production scheduling](/blog/what-is-production-scheduling), and identifying the resource that really limits throughput is [production bottleneck identification](/blog/production-bottleneck-identification). In a regulated device line, that resource is often a person, and the limit is their hours.

This post is the hours-and-capacity companion to [operator certifications for medical devices](/blog/edgebic-for-medical-devices-operator-certifications). Certifications establish who may do the work; the ledger establishes how much of it they can do in a day.

## Certification is a yes; hours are a how-much

A pure certification check answers one question: does a qualified person exist for this step? That is essential in a device shop, where only certified operators may perform validated processes. But it has a blind spot. If the answer is simply yes, then five jobs that all need the same certified inspector all pass the check and all schedule at once, sharing one person who can only be in one place. The plan looks staffed and is not.

The consequence shows up as a queue at the bench. The plan promised parallel inspections that were never possible, the floor serializes them by hand, and every downstream date built on the fiction slips. Presence checking overcommits people precisely because it never asks how many hours they have.

## The per-operator hours ledger

The fix is to track hours, not just presence. The engine keeps a ledger of each operator's hours keyed by day and shift. When it schedules a step that needs a skill, it debits the qualified operator's hours as it books work, and it only offers a window where a qualified, rostered, not-on-leave operator still has time. It caps what it commits at what the pool can afford, so a day can never oversell a person.

Now the everyday overcommit is impossible. Two jobs that both need the same certified inspector on Monday cannot both land Monday if she only has eight hours; the second moves to Tuesday, in the plan, before it becomes a bench queue. The certification requirement is unchanged, but the capacity reality that presence alone missed is now enforced. A multi-skilled operator counts once, not once per skill, because the ledger tracks the person's hours, not a separate pool per qualification.

## Hours that span shifts, without breaking the plan

Device operations can run longer than one shift, and the ledger has to handle that without collapsing. The engine separates two decisions. A gate asks whether a shift is usable at all: a shift with any staffed hours passes. Then an allocation clamp decides how much to book there: no more than the operator pool affords on that window.

That split is what makes long jobs schedulable. Take a 12-hour operation, one qualified operator working eight-hour days.

| Day | Operator hours | Booked | Remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | 8 | 8 | 4 |
| Tuesday | 8 | 4 | 0 |

Monday takes eight, Tuesday takes the remaining four, and the job finishes at midday. If the gate had instead rejected any shift where the full remaining work exceeded one day's staffing, every eight-hour shift would fail a twelve-hour job and the operation would be unschedulable despite a week of staffed hours. The gate stays permissive; the clamp keeps it honest.

## Crews and partial attention

Not every device operation needs one operator for its whole run, and some need two. The run attention figure captures that. One point zero is fully attended, one operator-hour per machine-hour. Two point zero is a two-person operation: each machine-hour books two operator-hours, and with a single qualified operator on shift the operation's day is capped at that person's hours divided by two. A half means one person tends two machines. Setup always books one full operator, because one person sets a machine up; the attention figure scales only the run phase. The full concept is defined in [what attend fraction means in operator scheduling](/blog/what-is-attend-fraction-operator-scheduling).

For a validated two-person assembly step, setting the attention to two point zero makes the plan reserve two qualified people for the duration and limit the operation to what your certified pool can staff. That is a plan that respects the validation, not one that quietly assumes a single operator can do a two-person job.

## Leave that moves work, not a silent substitution

When a certified operator takes leave, you enter it as an inclusive date range on that operator. On the next scheduling run, any step that depends on their hours and has no other qualified operator slides past the leave to their first day back. If a step is pinned to that specific person, because validation ties the work to a named, qualified individual, it slides through the absence rather than being handed to someone uncertified.

For a regulated manufacturer, that last property is the important one. The plan never substitutes an unqualified person to hit a date. A staffing gap becomes visible schedule movement that a planner sees and approves, which is exactly the traceable, defensible behavior a device shop needs. It sits naturally alongside the [audit trail](/blog/edgebic-for-medical-devices-audit-trail) and [routing snapshots](/blog/edgebic-for-medical-devices-routing-snapshots) that preserve what each job was built to and who changed what.

## Setting up operator-hours scheduling

Three steps get you there. Define the skills your validated processes require, and certify the operators qualified on each, with expiry dates so lapsed certifications drop out of the plan automatically. Roster the shifts each operator works, and enter their leave as it comes up. Then set the attention on each skill-bearing step: one for fully attended work, more for crews, a fraction where one person covers several stations.

When no operator data is loaded, or on steps with no skill, every check does nothing and the plan behaves as a machine-only plan would, so you add the labor constraint precisely where regulation requires it.
