**The proven way to adopt EDGEBIC is a pilot on one cell: import that cell's data, verify capacity, schedule it, compare the dates, and expand only when the planners trust the result.** In EDGEBIC by User Solutions, a single-cell pilot keeps your current system as the system of record while EDGEBIC proves itself on a small, controlled area. Because EDGEBIC imports from Excel and CSV, standing up a pilot cell needs no special infrastructure, and there is no forced timeline. You widen the pilot cell by cell, at the pace your team earns confidence.

## Why one cell, not the whole plant

A scheduling migration fails when it is too big to control. Switch the whole plant at once and any modeling difference between the old system and the new one surfaces as a production problem, at a scale where you cannot tell which of a hundred changes caused it.

A one-cell pilot inverts that. You get a fair, contained test of the engine on an area small enough to reason about, while the rest of the plant runs undisturbed on your current system. Differences are visible and explainable, not buried in noise. And because nothing the plant depends on is at stake, the pilot is low-pressure, which is exactly the environment in which planners actually engage with a new tool. This phased approach is the one built into the [RMDB to EDGEBIC upgrade path](/rmdb-to-edgebic).

## Choosing the cell

The right pilot cell is important enough to matter and contained enough to control:

- **Self-contained.** A work area or product family whose routings mostly stay inside the cell, so you are not importing half the plant to schedule it.
- **A clear bottleneck.** A cell with a real constraint shows off finite capacity scheduling and gives the comparison something meaningful to test. It also lets the planner see the engine handle the exact problem they care about.
- **Stable routings.** Pick an area whose routings are reasonably clean, so the pilot tests the engine, not your data hygiene.
- **An engaged planner.** Someone who will look at the comparison each cycle and reconcile differences. The pilot's value is the comparison habit, and that needs a person.

Resist starting with the most chaotic corner of the shop. A first pilot should be a fair test of the engine, not a stress test of the migration.

## The five steps

A pilot is a short, repeatable loop.

**1. Import the cell's data.** Export the cell's products, work centers, routings, and open orders from your current system to Excel or CSV, and import them into EDGEBIC through import masks. Map each entity once and save the mask; the routing import wires step sequences automatically. The mechanics are in [moving your RMDB routings into EDGEBIC](/blog/moving-rmdb-routings-into-edgebic) and [EDGEBIC import masks explained](/blog/edgebic-import-masks-explained).

**2. Verify capacity and calendars.** Set the cell's shifts, holidays, and per-work-center calendars, then check the capacity numbers against what your current system shows. This step catches the most common source of date differences before it can confuse the comparison.

**3. Schedule the cell.** Run a scheduling pass. EDGEBIC places the cell's jobs against real finite capacity.

**4. Compare the dates.** Line up EDGEBIC's dates against your existing plan for the same jobs. Most will match. Where they differ, reconcile: a difference almost always traces to a shift calendar, a capacity number, or a routing time modeled differently, and fixing the input converges the two systems.

**5. Expand when trust is earned.** When the planners stop double-checking EDGEBIC's dates for the cell, add a second cell and repeat the loop.

## A worked example

A shop pilots EDGEBIC on its CNC cell: three machines, a clear bottleneck at the mill, about 40 active jobs.

1. **Import.** The planner exports the cell's products, work centers, routings, and orders, and imports them through four saved masks in an afternoon.
2. **Verify.** They set the Day Shift calendar and the mill's capacity, and compare against the current system. One number is off; they correct it.
3. **Schedule.** EDGEBIC schedules the cell. The bottleneck at the mill shows clearly on the Gantt.
4. **Compare.** Over three cycles, EDGEBIC's dates are checked against the old plan. Early differences trace to a holiday the calendar was missing; once added, the dates converge.
5. **Expand.** By the fourth cycle the planner trusts the cell's dates without cross-checking. Management approves a second cell.

The planner time was minutes per cycle. The current system stayed in charge the whole time. Nothing the plant depended on was ever at risk.

## Keep the current system in charge

Throughout the pilot, your existing system remains the system of record. The plant runs on its schedule; the EDGEBIC cell is a parallel comparison. This is what makes the pilot safe, and it is why there is no forced cutover: RMDB and the rest of your setup stay fully supported for as long as you want them. The transition completes only when EDGEBIC owns the schedule cell by cell and the old system has nothing left to prove. The parallel-run discipline behind this is covered in [running RMDB and EDGEBIC side by side](/blog/running-rmdb-and-edgebic-side-by-side).

## Knowing when it worked

Success is not a date on a plan; it is a change in behavior. Early in a pilot, planners verify every EDGEBIC date against the old system. As differences get reconciled and confidence grows, they check less. When they trust the cell's schedule without cross-checking, the pilot has succeeded, and that is the signal to widen it. Chasing a fixed go-live date instead of that trust signal is how migrations get forced before they are ready.

## The takeaway

Plan an EDGEBIC pilot as one cell and a comparison habit: choose a self-contained area with a clear bottleneck, import its data through masks, verify capacity, schedule, and compare dates each cycle. Keep your current system as the system of record, reconcile differences as modeling insights, and expand only when the planners trust the result. The effort is minutes per cycle, the risk is contained, and there is no forced cutover.
