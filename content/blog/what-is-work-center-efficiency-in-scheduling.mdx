**Work center efficiency is the percentage of a machine's nominal, rated capacity that it actually delivers in practice.** A machine seldom produces exactly what its nameplate promises, and efficiency captures the gap. In a schedule, efficiency scales the machine's usable output so the plan is built on achievable throughput rather than an optimistic rating. EDGEBIC by User Solutions folds efficiency into every work center's capacity math so a slower machine schedules like a slower machine, not like its faster twin.

## How it works

Efficiency is a percentage attached to each work center. At 100%, the machine delivers its full rated capacity. Below 100%, it delivers proportionally less: an older machine consistently running at 85% of its rating effectively provides less usable output than its nameplate, and the schedule accounts for that by treating its capacity as reduced.

The important thing is that efficiency describes the machine's running speed, not how busy the plan keeps it. Two levers turn a work center's raw shift hours into the effective hours the engine can book. Efficiency is one: how much of its rated speed the machine actually delivers. The available-capacity percentage is the other: how much of the shift you deliberately reserve for productive scheduling versus changeovers, minor stoppages, and breathing room. Keeping them separate stops one number from doing two jobs.

Because efficiency scales output, it shapes finish dates quietly. The same routing on a 100% machine and an 85% machine will not finish at the same time, and that is the point: the plan reflects the real difference between the two.

## A concrete example

Think of a copier rated at 40 pages a minute that, in daily use, reliably turns out 36. It is not broken and it is not idle; it simply delivers 90% of its rating when it runs. That 90% is its efficiency. If you planned a big print run assuming 40 a minute, you would come up short every time. Planning at 36 a minute matches reality.

Now compare that to whether the copier is busy at all. It might sit unused for hours, then run a job at its honest 36 a minute. Its efficiency is still 90% whether the job lasts five minutes or five hours; how much of the day it spends running is a separate question, and that is utilization, not efficiency.

In the plant, an older mill rated identically to a newer one but delivering 85% of that rating should carry 85% efficiency. Scheduled work then consumes more of its rated time, and the finish date is honest instead of optimistic.
