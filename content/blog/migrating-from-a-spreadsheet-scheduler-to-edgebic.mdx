**Migrating from a homegrown spreadsheet scheduler to EDGEBIC means mapping your workbook's columns to built-in import masks, so your existing tabular data moves in without re-entry while the engine replaces your fragile formulas with finite capacity scheduling.** In EDGEBIC by User Solutions, you split the workbook into work centers, routings, and orders, save each as CSV, and map the columns once per entity. Your data carries forward. The formulas that only one person understood do not, because the engine enforces the rules those formulas were trying to approximate.

## The spreadsheet did its job, and hit its limit

A homegrown scheduling workbook is a sign of a capable team. Someone built a tool that fit the shop when nothing off the shelf did. The trouble is what happens as it grows: formulas pile up, one person becomes the only one who understands them, and the workbook cannot actually enforce finite capacity. It shows you a plan, but nothing stops two jobs landing on the same machine in the same hour. The human catches that by hand, with colors and notes and overrides.

Moving to EDGEBIC keeps the data your team captured and replaces the fragile logic with an engine that enforces the rules. The good news is that your data is already tabular, so the migration is mostly a mapping exercise rather than the from-scratch capture a [whiteboard shop faces](/blog/migrating-from-a-whiteboard-to-edgebic).

## This is not the same as an RMX move

If you run RMX, Resource Manager for Excel, from User Solutions, your migration follows a documented path because RMX has a known structure. That is covered in [moving from Excel RMX to EDGEBIC](/blog/from-excel-rmx-to-edgebic). A homegrown workbook is different: it has whatever structure its author chose, so you have one extra step. You audit your own columns and decide what each one means before you map it. The import masks are the same; the decision about what feeds them is yours to make.

## Separate the data from the logic

The single most useful move is to split the workbook into two categories: data you keep, and logic you retire.

**Data you keep** is anything factual: a work center name, a machine's daily hours, an operation sequence, a run time, an order quantity, a due date. This maps to EDGEBIC master data.

**Logic you retire** is the formulas, the conditional colors, the manual date overrides, and the notes that told the human what the tool could not enforce. None of this imports, and none of it should. The engine replaces it.

Being honest about which columns are which is the audit that de-risks the whole move. A column that computes a finish date is logic; the run time it used is data. Keep the run time, drop the formula.

## Mapping columns to masks

Once you have the data columns identified, split them into three flat tables and map each to its mask.

| Your workbook | EDGEBIC entity | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Machine list, hours per day, number of units | Work centers | Capacity fields feed the finite capacity engine |
| Product, operation order, work center per step, run and setup time | Routings | Two-pass routing import wires the step sequence |
| Job, quantity, due date, customer | Manufacturing orders | Due dates drive scheduling |
| Working days, holidays, downtime | Shifts and calendars | Configured in the application, not imported |

Save each table as CSV, then map its columns to the mask fields once. EDGEBIC does not require your column names to match anything; you point each source column at the field it feeds. Clean your data first so the mapping does not carry old errors forward, using the same discipline in [cleaning your data before importing to EDGEBIC](/blog/cleaning-your-data-before-importing-to-edgebic).

## What the engine gives you that formulas could not

The reason to leave the workbook behind is what EDGEBIC enforces automatically.

**Finite capacity.** The engine never plans two jobs on the same machine at once. Your workbook could show that mistake but not prevent it.

**Whole-plant reschedule.** When a machine goes down or a hot order arrives, you rerun and get a new plan in seconds that still respects every constraint, instead of dragging cells around and hoping the formulas still hold.

**Routings and calendars applied automatically.** The engine walks every operation in sequence and honors your shift calendar without a single formula.

**A flagged bottleneck.** EDGEBIC identifies the constraint work center and schedules around it, which a spreadsheet leaves entirely to the human.

**Completed work stays put.** On a reschedule, work already done is preserved and only future operations move, so the plan resumes from where the shop actually is.

## Run them side by side first

Do not switch cold. Keep the workbook running and schedule the same orders in EDGEBIC for a couple of weeks, then compare. Where the plans differ, understand why. Usually EDGEBIC caught a capacity conflict the workbook let through, which is exactly the reassurance the team needs to trust the new plan. This [parallel-run approach](/blog/running-rmdb-and-edgebic-side-by-side) is how you retire the file that only one person understood without a leap of faith.

## The takeaway

To migrate from a homegrown spreadsheet scheduler to EDGEBIC, separate the data you keep from the logic you retire, split the data into work centers, routings, and orders, and map each table's columns to the built-in import masks once. Your tabular data moves in without re-entry, shifts are configured in the application, and the engine replaces your fragile formulas with enforced finite capacity. Run both in parallel until the team trusts the new plan.
