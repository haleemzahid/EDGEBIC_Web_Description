**A skill in manufacturing scheduling is a named certification or capability, such as TIG-Inconel or CMM Inspection, that a routing step can require so the scheduler only staffs the step when a qualified operator is available.** It takes the framed certificate on the shop wall, the one that says who is allowed to run a given operation, and turns it into a hard constraint the engine must honor. A step with a required skill will only be placed in a window where a certified, rostered, not-on-leave operator still has unbooked hours.

## How a Skill Works

A skill lives in a catalog as a named capability. On its own it does nothing; it becomes a constraint the moment a routing step declares it as its required skill. That declaration is the whole mechanism. The engine now models two resources for that step, not one: the machine is the primary resource, and a qualified human is the second resource that must also be free at the same time.

When the scheduler considers a window for a skilled step, it checks the machine's capacity as always, then checks whether any operator who is certified for the required skill, rostered on that shift, and not on time off, has hours still available. If both conditions hold, the step can run; if the qualified pool is exhausted for that window, the step waits for the next window where a certified operator has room.

Skills are the reason a plant with plenty of machine hours can still be short on capacity. Steel does not care who runs the press, but an Inconel weld does care who holds the torch, and a skill is how EDGEBIC encodes that difference.

## A Concrete Example

Take a plant with three TIG welders. Two are certified for mild steel only; one, Joe, also holds the Inconel certificate. Without a skill, the engine sees a Welding work center with three interchangeable people and will happily schedule three Inconel jobs to run Monday.

Now create a skill called TIG-Inconel, certify Joe against it, and set it as the required skill on the Inconel welding routing steps. On the next scheduling run the engine treats those steps as needing Joe or another Inconel-certified welder. Since Joe is the only one, the three Inconel jobs cannot all run Monday: there is only one Joe, so two of them move to the next windows where he has free hours. The mild-steel jobs, whose steps carry no required skill, keep flowing across all three welders exactly as before.

That is the framed welding certificate on the wall, turned into a schedulable constraint. The plan now reflects who is actually allowed to do the work, not just how many machines exist.

## How EDGEBIC Uses It

A skill is master data you maintain in the skill catalog, and it is referenced by a routing step's required-skill field. Several product behaviors flow from that single link:

- **The scheduling engine** builds its labor feed from active certifications only. When it constructs the qualified pool for a step, expired or inactive certifications are filtered out, so an operator whose certificate lapsed stops counting as qualified on the very next run.
- **Routing steps** opt in per step. A step with no required skill is unconstrained by labor, which keeps the feature backward compatible with every routing built before skills existed.
- **The delete guard** protects data integrity. A skill still required by steps cannot be hard-deleted; deactivate it instead, and it drops out of future selection without breaking historical plans.

A skill pairs naturally with an operator's [attend fraction](/blog/what-is-attend-fraction-operator-scheduling), which controls how much of an operator's time a running step actually consumes once a qualified person is assigned. Read the two together for the full picture of how EDGEBIC schedules people as a [secondary resource](/blog/what-is-a-secondary-resource-in-manufacturing-scheduling) that has to be free at the same moment as the machine, and use the [how to require a skill on a routing step](/blog/how-to-require-a-skill-on-a-routing-step-in-edgebic) recipe to switch the constraint on for a specific operation.

No. A skill that is still required by one or more routing steps cannot be hard-deleted, because doing so would orphan those steps' labor constraint.
