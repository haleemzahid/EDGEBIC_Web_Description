**A manufacturing scheduling glossary** is only useful if it defines terms the way the software actually behaves, not the way a textbook wishes it did. This index organizes the EDGEBIC glossary by theme, so you can find the definition you need by where the term lives in the work rather than by guessing its first letter.

Every entry is written the same way: a standalone definition first, then how it works, then a concrete example, then how EDGEBIC by User Solutions uses it. Below are the most-consulted terms grouped by subject area. The full glossary runs to several hundred entries and is browsable from the [blog index](/blogs); this page is the map, not the territory.

A word on why the vocabulary is as large as it is. Scheduling terminology accumulates in layers. Some terms are industry-standard and predate any software: bill of materials, setup time, lead time. Some come from a body of theory that the engine implements directly, such as the drum, the buffer, and the rope of constraint management. And some exist only because a finite capacity scheduler has to model a distinction that a simpler system never makes, which is why words like machine instance, setup family, and transfer batch have no equivalent in most ERPs. Where a term is generic, the definition says so and stays generic. Where EDGEBIC gives it a specific meaning, the entry says that too, because the gap between the textbook and the software is exactly where planners get caught.

## The Scheduling Engine

These are the terms that describe how a plan gets built. If you read only one group, read this one.

The direction decision comes first: [forward scheduling](/blog/what-is-forward-scheduling-in-edgebic) starts from now and pushes work as early as capacity allows, [backward scheduling](/blog/what-is-slack-time-in-forward-vs-backward-scheduling) works back from a due date, and [direction precedence](/blog/what-is-direction-precedence-in-scheduling) is the rule that decides which applies to a given job. A [scheduling mode](/blog/what-is-a-scheduling-mode) names the whole family.

The core constraint is [finite capacity](/blog/what-does-finite-capacity-mean-in-edgebic), whose opposite is [infinite capacity planning](/blog/what-is-infinite-capacity-planning). Work is placed by a [greedy scheduling](/blog/what-is-greedy-scheduling-in-manufacturing) pass over a [dependency graph](/blog/what-is-a-dependency-graph-in-scheduling), ordered by [topological sort](/blog/what-is-a-topological-sort-in-job-scheduling), which is why job order matters so much.

Routing structure has its own vocabulary: a [bill of routing](/blog/what-is-a-bill-of-routing) with its [sequence numbers](/blog/what-is-a-sequence-number-in-a-routing), [setup time](/blog/what-is-setup-time-in-manufacturing), [run time per unit](/blog/what-is-run-time-per-unit-in-manufacturing-scheduling), [queue time](/blog/what-is-queue-time-in-manufacturing-scheduling), [move time](/blog/what-is-move-time-in-a-routing), and [transit time](/blog/what-is-transit-time-in-production-scheduling).

Advanced routing behavior is where most of the depth lives: [lot streaming](/blog/what-is-lot-streaming) and its [transfer batch](/blog/what-is-a-transfer-batch), [FlowStep overlap](/blog/what-is-flowstep-overlap), [independent parallel processing](/blog/what-is-independent-parallel-processing) versus [dependent parallel processing](/blog/what-is-dependent-parallel-processing), a [true alternate work center](/blog/what-is-a-true-alternate-work-center), and an [anchor step](/blog/what-is-an-anchor-step-in-scheduling).

Constraint theory terms appear because EDGEBIC implements them: [theory of constraints](/blog/what-is-theory-of-constraints), [drum-buffer-rope](/blog/what-is-drum-buffer-rope-in-manufacturing), the [constraint buffer](/blog/what-is-a-constraint-buffer), the [shipping buffer](/blog/what-is-a-shipping-buffer-in-toc-scheduling), and the [bottleneck work center](/blog/what-is-a-bottleneck-work-center) they all revolve around.

Dispatch rules that name an ordering: [earliest due date](/blog/what-is-earliest-due-date-in-scheduling), [shortest processing time](/blog/what-is-shortest-processing-time-in-scheduling), [longest processing time](/blog/what-is-longest-processing-time-in-scheduling), and [critical ratio](/blog/what-is-critical-ratio-in-scheduling).

And the measures of a finished plan: [makespan](/blog/what-is-makespan-in-scheduling), [tardiness](/blog/what-is-tardiness-in-scheduling), [days late](/blog/what-is-days-late-on-a-job), an [idle gap](/blog/what-is-an-idle-gap-in-a-schedule), and [schedule nervousness](/blog/what-is-schedule-nervousness).

## Capacity, Work Centers, and Calendars

Where the hours come from, and how they run out.

[Available capacity](/blog/what-is-available-capacity-in-scheduling) is the starting point, compared against demand as [load vs capacity](/blog/what-is-load-vs-capacity-in-scheduling) and expressed as [capacity utilization](/blog/what-is-capacity-utilization-in-scheduling). A [work center type](/blog/what-is-a-work-center-type) and its [capacity type](/blog/what-is-capacity-type-in-a-work-center) decide how a resource behaves, and [pieces per hour](/blog/what-is-pieces-per-hour-in-work-center-capacity) is the alternative to an hours-based rate.

Multiple machines behind one name introduce the [machine instance](/blog/what-is-a-machine-instance-in-scheduling), [per-instance capacity](/blog/what-is-per-instance-capacity-in-scheduling), an [instance selection strategy](/blog/what-is-an-instance-selection-strategy-in-scheduling), and the [instance collision](/blog/what-is-an-instance-collision) that signals a modeling error.

Pooled machines are handled by a [work center group](/blog/what-is-a-work-center-group-machine-pool), whose members carry a [factor from base](/blog/what-is-factor-from-base-in-work-center-groups) against the [nominal primary](/blog/what-is-nominal-primary-in-work-center-groups). [Group explosion](/blog/what-is-group-explosion-in-scheduling) and [re-shopping](/blog/what-is-re-shopping-in-work-center-group-scheduling) describe how a group is resolved at schedule time, and a [selection strategy](/blog/what-is-a-selection-strategy-in-scheduling) decides which member wins.

Time itself: a [work center shift](/blog/what-is-a-work-center-shift), [shift time](/blog/what-is-shift-time-in-production-scheduling), the [shift priority score](/blog/what-is-a-shift-priority-score-in-scheduling), an [overnight shift wrap-around](/blog/what-is-an-overnight-shift-wrap-around-in-scheduling), a [cross-shift operation](/blog/what-is-a-cross-shift-operation-in-scheduling), a [plant holiday](/blog/what-is-a-plant-holiday-in-scheduling) with its [holiday scope](/blog/what-is-holiday-scope-in-scheduling-calendars), and the difference between a [working day and a calendar day](/blog/working-day-vs-calendar-day-in-scheduling).

Two settings that are frequently confused: [work center efficiency](/blog/what-is-work-center-efficiency-in-scheduling) and the [schedule-at-utilization setting](/blog/what-is-the-schedule-at-utilization-setting), which is set per job.

## Shop Floor Execution

What the floor reports back, and what the schedule does with it.

The [shop floor kiosk](/blog/what-is-a-shop-floor-kiosk) captures work as punches: a [punch type](/blog/what-is-a-punch-type-in-shop-floor-tracking), a [punch source](/blog/what-is-a-punch-source-in-shop-floor-tracking), and a [punch adjustment](/blog/what-is-a-punch-adjustment). A [partial completion](/blog/what-is-a-partial-completion-in-scheduling) and [mark complete](/blog/what-does-mark-complete-mean-on-an-operation) close a step out.

Replanning around recorded work: a [reschedule](/blog/what-is-a-reschedule-in-scheduling), [actuals preservation on reschedule](/blog/what-is-actuals-preservation-on-reschedule), a [frozen window](/blog/what-is-a-frozen-window-in-scheduling), a [targeted reschedule](/blog/what-is-a-targeted-reschedule), and [routing drift](/blog/what-is-routing-drift-in-production-scheduling).

Operators: a [skill](/blog/what-is-a-skill-in-manufacturing-scheduling), an [operator certification](/blog/what-is-an-operator-certification-in-scheduling), a [shift operator roster](/blog/what-is-a-shift-operator-roster), [operator time off](/blog/what-is-operator-time-off-in-scheduling), and [attend fraction](/blog/what-is-attend-fraction-operator-scheduling), which is how one operator covers several machines.

## Data, Import, and Setup

[Import mask](/blog/what-is-an-import-mask) is the term to know first, followed by the [import entity type](/blog/what-is-an-import-entity-type) it belongs to, the [import row status](/blog/what-is-an-import-row-status) each row receives, and the [import run log](/blog/what-is-an-import-run-log) that records the outcome. An [upsert](/blog/what-is-an-upsert-in-data-import) explains the create-or-update behavior, a [conversion factor](/blog/what-is-a-conversion-factor-in-data-import) turns minutes into hours, and [row-error tolerance](/blog/what-is-row-error-tolerance-in-a-data-import) decides how much bad data aborts a run. A [grid export snapshot](/blog/what-is-a-grid-export-snapshot) is the export side, and it deliberately does not round-trip.

Changeover setup: a [setup family](/blog/what-is-a-setup-family-sequence-dependent-changeover), a [setup matrix entry](/blog/what-is-a-setup-matrix-entry), and the [setup source](/blog/what-is-a-setup-source-in-scheduling) that decides which value applies.

## Reading the Schedule on Screen

The visual layer has vocabulary of its own, and it is worth learning because most planners meet these terms as colors and shapes before they meet them as words. A [Gantt baseline](/blog/what-is-a-gantt-baseline) is the saved comparison line that shows how far a plan has moved. An [operation state in a production Gantt](/blog/what-is-an-operation-state-in-a-production-gantt) is what the color of a bar means. A [job display mode](/blog/what-is-a-job-display-mode-in-the-gantt) and a [timeline view mode](/blog/what-is-a-timeline-view-mode-in-the-gantt) control how much of the plan is on screen at once, and a [drag write mode](/blog/what-is-a-drag-write-mode-in-a-production-gantt) decides what happens when you move a bar with the mouse. Alongside them, a [work center dispatch list](/blog/what-is-a-work-center-dispatch-list) is the plan as the floor sees it, and a [KPI tile](/blog/what-is-a-kpi-tile-in-a-manufacturing-dashboard) is the plan as management sees it.

Two more that come up in conversation constantly: the [original scheduled date](/blog/what-is-an-original-scheduled-date-in-a-production-schedule), which is what a job was promised before anything moved, and the [schedule change log](/blog/what-is-a-schedule-change-log), which is the record of what moved and when.

## Terms That Describe How the System Behaves

A small group of terms describe the software's own rules rather than manufacturing. They are worth knowing because they explain behavior that otherwise looks arbitrary. [Last-write-wins concurrency](/blog/what-is-last-write-wins-concurrency) explains what happens when two planners save at once. [Atomic rollback](/blog/what-is-atomic-rollback-in-production-scheduling) explains why a failed scheduling run leaves nothing half-written. [Override and warn](/blog/what-is-override-and-warn-in-production-scheduling) describes the pattern EDGEBIC uses instead of a hard block, where you are told the consequence and allowed to proceed. A [security role](/blog/what-is-a-security-role-in-manufacturing-software) is how access is granted, and permissions come from roles.
