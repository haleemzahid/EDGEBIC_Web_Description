**A shift priority score is a number computed for every candidate shift before any hours are booked, expressing how attractive that shift is for the operation being placed.** It weights three things in descending order of importance: whether the shift can start at or before the moment the operation becomes eligible, how much free capacity it offers from that moment, and how early in the day it begins. The score is a preference, not a schedule; a second sort by date is what determines the order the engine actually walks. EDGEBIC by User Solutions computes it for each candidate the day-by-day capacity search turns up.

## How it works

Placing one operation begins with a search. The engine walks forward day by day from the moment the operation becomes eligible, and for each day it examines every shift the work center is associated with. Shifts that fail validation are discarded before scoring: a shift that ends before the eligible moment offers nothing, and so does one whose remaining hours fall below the minimum worth allocating.

Whatever survives gets scored, and three terms contribute.

**Immediate availability, weighted heavily.** A shift that can begin at or before the moment the operation becomes eligible receives a large fixed bonus. One that cannot receives nothing for this term. This is the dominant factor by a wide margin, and it encodes a simple principle: starting now beats starting later, almost regardless of what else is on offer.

**Free capacity, weighted moderately.** The score rises in proportion to the hours available from the eligible moment onward, at a modest multiplier. Note that this is capacity from that moment, not the shift's total free capacity, which matters for an operation becoming eligible mid-shift.

**Time of day, weighted lightly.** A small term prefers shifts that begin earlier in the day. This is essentially a tie-breaker, and its practical effect is that when two shifts on the same day are otherwise comparable, the earlier one is preferred.

Scoring produces a ranking, and then a second sort runs over the same candidates by date ascending, then shift, then available capacity descending. This is the part that surprises people. The date sort means the engine always considers earlier days before later ones, so a very high scoring shift on Tuesday will never jump ahead of a lower-scoring one on Monday. The score decides preference within what the calendar makes possible; the date sort ensures the calendar wins.

## A concrete example

A work center runs two shifts: a day shift from 08:00 to 16:00 and a night shift from 20:00 to 04:00. An operation becomes eligible on Monday at 08:00, and both shifts have eight free hours.

**Monday day shift.** It can start at or before 08:00, so it collects the large availability bonus of 1000. Eight free hours contribute 80. Starting at hour eight of the day contributes 92. Total: 1172.

**Monday night shift.** It starts at 20:00, which is after the operation became eligible, so it collects nothing for availability. Eight free hours contribute 80. Starting at hour twenty contributes 80. Total: 160.

The gap is not close, and it is not close because of capacity. Both shifts offer identical hours. The entire difference is the availability bonus plus a small time-of-day preference. A night shift would need an implausible amount of extra free capacity to overhaul a day shift that can start immediately, which is precisely the behavior a planner wants: an operation ready to run at eight in the morning should run at eight in the morning, not wait twelve hours for a roomier slot.

Now extend to Tuesday. Tuesday's day shift scores well too, and might even outscore Monday's night shift. But the date sort places both Monday shifts ahead of it regardless, so the engine fills Monday day, then Monday night, then Tuesday day. Whatever the scores say, the walk goes forward in time.

## How EDGEBIC uses it

Scoring sits inside the multi-shift allocator, between finding candidate shifts and booking hours into them. It is not a setting and it is not exposed, because it is not a policy decision so much as a formalization of what any planner would do by hand: take the slot you can start in now, prefer the one with more room, and prefer earlier in the day when it makes no difference. The search it orders is described in [how shift priority scoring orders the capacity search](/blog/how-shift-priority-scoring-orders-the-capacity-search).

The scored candidates are what the allocator then fills, splitting an operation across as many of them as its hours require. That behavior is covered in [how EDGEBIC allocates hours across shifts](/blog/how-edgebic-allocates-hours-across-shifts) and in [what is a cross-shift operation in scheduling](/blog/what-is-a-cross-shift-operation-in-scheduling).

Two inputs to the score deserve attention because they are the ones a planner actually controls. What a shift reports as free is described in [what is available capacity in scheduling](/blog/what-is-available-capacity-in-scheduling), and it is not simply total hours minus booked hours once an operation becomes eligible partway through a shift. And the slot being scored is the structure described in [what is a shift resource allocation in scheduling](/blog/what-is-a-shift-resource-allocation-in-scheduling). For the wider allocation picture see [EDGEBIC multi-shift scheduling explained](/blog/edgebic-multi-shift-scheduling-explained).

## The takeaway

A shift priority score answers which of these shifts would I rather use, and the answer is dominated by one question: can it start now. Capacity and time of day matter, but neither comes close to overturning immediate availability, which is why a schedule fills days before nights and earlier days before later ones even when the later slots look roomier. When work lands somewhere unexpected, the productive question is almost never about the ranking and almost always about the inputs: what did that shift actually offer from the moment the operation became eligible, and did anything remove hours the grid still appears to show.
