**A schedule is only as honest as the actuals feeding it, and a sheet filled in from memory at the end of a shift is the weakest link in the plant's data. EDGEBIC by User Solutions replaces that sheet with a floor kiosk where the operator records start and stop at the machine, so the actuals are captured once, at the source, and land straight on the schedule.** The clipboard, the clerk, and the morning re-keying all go away.

This post explains what the kiosk captures, the specific data errors it removes, and where its limits are. For how those actuals then improve the plan, see [how kiosk actuals close the planning loop](/blog/how-kiosk-actuals-close-the-planning-loop). This post sits under the [EDGEBIC results guide](/blog/edgebic-results-guide).

## The Hidden Cost of the Paper Route

The paper route looks free because nobody bills for it, but it costs the plant in three places every single day.

**The operator's memory.** A sheet completed at 3 p.m. asks the operator to recall when each of the day's jobs started and stopped. By the third job that is a reconstruction, not a record, and the reconstruction always rounds to tidy numbers that never quite happened.

**The clerk's morning.** Someone collects the sheets, reads them, and types them in. That is an hour or more of skilled clerical time spent copying data that already exists, plus every misread digit and every ambiguous scrawl becoming a wrong number in the system.

**The plan's foundation.** The reschedule the planner runs is built on those numbers. If the actuals are a day old and rounded from memory, the new plan starts from a fiction, and no engine can schedule its way out of bad input.

The paper is cheap. The three costs behind it are not.

## What the Kiosk Captures

EDGEBIC's kiosk is a dedicated floor station that shares the same database as the planning application. The operator sees the live schedule, taps the job in front of them, and records the work as it happens:

- **Actual start** when the job begins, as a real clock time.
- **Actual end** when it finishes, again as a real clock time.
- **Hours** against the job, which flow into the daily breakdown the schedule keeps.

Because the kiosk writes to the same schedule the planner reads, there is no export, no merge, and no reconciliation between a floor list and an office list. The job the operator punches is the job the planner sees. Captured once, used everywhere.

The design constraint that matters is speed. Recording a punch has to be faster than writing a paper line, because a data-capture step that is slower than paper will quietly lose to paper. Tap the job, tap start, tap stop.

## The Errors That Disappear

Three classes of error go away, and they are the three that most corrupt a schedule:

| Error class | On paper | With kiosk capture |
|---|---|---|
| Recall error | Times reconstructed at shift end | Real clock time recorded live |
| Transcription error | Clerk re-keys a handwritten sheet | No second copy exists |
| Latency | Data lands the next morning | Data lands as the work happens |

Notice what these have in common: none of them are the operator being careless. They are structural properties of asking a person to remember, a second person to copy, and everyone to wait. Remove the memory step, remove the copy step, and remove the wait, and the errors have nowhere to live.

Accurate actuals are not a reporting nicety. They are what make the next reschedule believable, because a plan rebuilt on real start and stop times reflects the floor, while a plan rebuilt on rounded recall reflects a guess. Completed work is never moved by a reschedule, so the actuals you capture become a permanent, trustworthy record of what happened, not a number that gets overwritten.

## Where the Clerk's Hour Goes

The clerical hour that used to go to transcription does not vanish from the payroll; it goes to work that needs a human. Chasing a genuinely missing punch, checking an outlier, or helping the planner read the load is worth a person's time. Copying legible numbers from a sheet into a screen is not. The kiosk moves the effort from the copying no one should do to the judgment only a person can.

This is the same shape as the broader clerical saving a scheduling system delivers, covered in [how a scheduler pays for itself in a quarter](/blog/how-a-scheduler-pays-for-itself-in-a-quarter). The kiosk is the floor-level instance of it: one whole copy of the data, and the person who made that copy, freed for something better.

## The Honest Limits

**The kiosk cannot make an operator punch.** If a job runs and no one taps start, the actual is missing, and a missing punch is its own kind of bad data. The gain depends on the floor discipline of punching in and out, which is a habit to build, not a setting to switch on.

**It cannot fix a wrong job selection.** Tapping the wrong job records real time against the wrong work. The kiosk removes recall and transcription error, but it cannot remove the operator selecting the line above the one they meant. Clear job labeling on the floor still matters.

**It is not a substitute for the plan.** The kiosk records what happened. It does not decide what should happen next. The value is realized when those actuals feed the reschedule, which is a separate step covered in [closing the loop from shop floor to plan](/blog/closing-the-loop-from-shop-floor-to-plan).

**It does not run without the shared database.** The kiosk's whole advantage is that it writes to the same schedule the planner uses. That is a design strength, but it means the floor station and the planning application are one system, set up together, not two tools bolted at the edges.
