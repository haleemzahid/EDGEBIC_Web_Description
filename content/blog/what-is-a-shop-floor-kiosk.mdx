**A shop floor kiosk is a terminal on the production floor where operators record what is happening on their machine, starting a job, running it, pausing for a breakdown or missing material, and finishing, so that actuals flow back to the schedule.** It is the shop-floor equivalent of a time clock, except it clocks the job and the machine rather than the worker's shift. Each action an operator takes is a punch, and the punches roll up into the [actual dates](/blog/what-is-an-actual-date-in-scheduling) and hours the plan needs to stay honest.

## How a Shop Floor Kiosk Works

A schedule is only as good as its feedback. Without a way to record what actually happened, the plan drifts from reality within a shift, and the planner is guessing. The kiosk is the feedback channel: it sits where the work is, and it turns operator actions into structured data the scheduler can consume.

The unit of capture is the punch. A punch is a single state on one job at one machine over a span of time. The states cover the real life of an operation: setup while the machine is being prepared and no good pieces are made, run while it is producing, idle while the operator waits for material or the next job, down for a breakdown, rework for correcting a defect, and teardown, which despite its name is the *first* setup phase on the kiosk's changeover ribbon, where the previous job's setup is stripped before the new one is built.

Run punches carry output. As the machine produces, the kiosk accumulates good pieces, scrap, and rework separately, so the record reflects yield rather than just quantity. The terminal does not vary from machine to machine: the elapsed clock runs during production and the counters are there to tap at every station. Which measure leads is settled when the day is logged, where the missing side can derive from a rate or both can be entered independently so performance variance can be computed.

At end of day, the punches for each operation roll up: the day's hours are recomputed from the punch spans, and the operation's actual start and end are set. Those are exactly the values a reschedule reads.

## A Concrete Example

An operator arrives at CNC-3 for an 8-hour run. At the kiosk they:

- Punch **setup** while installing the fixture and loading tools.
- Punch **run** and begin producing; the kiosk counts good pieces and one scrap.
- Punch **down** mid-shift when a coolant line fails, selecting a machine reason code, then back to **run** when it is fixed.
- Tap **complete operation** to close the job. Teardown is not an end-of-job punch: it is the first phase of the next changeover.

The stamps land as they happen rather than in a nightly batch: the operation's actual start is written at the first start-run tap, and its actual end when the operator taps complete operation. Punches accumulate into a daily hour total that separates productive run time from downtime. Nothing auto-closes a punch left open, so a machine down overnight should simply be left paused, where the time is recorded as non-productive; a punch left running inflates its phase and needs a supervisor correction in the history drawer, which is recorded and attributed.

## How EDGEBIC Uses It

EDGEBIC ships a dedicated shop floor kiosk application that shares the same database as the planning desktop, so punches become actuals on the planner's schedule directly.

- **Operators punch state changes.** Setup, run, idle, down, rework, and teardown are captured per job per machine, with good, scrap, and rework pieces on run punches.
- **Hours or pieces lead.** The terminal captures both at every machine: the elapsed run clock advances on its own and the good and scrap counters are tapped per piece. Which measure a shop records as the truth is decided when the day is logged, through the Auto Calc Hours control, rather than configured per station.
- **The stamps land as they happen.** The first start-run tap stamps the operation's actual start and completing the operation stamps its actual end, with punches accumulating into per-day hours; an end-of-day snap keeps a same-day close from producing an inverted date.
- **Corrections are auditable.** Auto-close handles forgotten punches, and supervisor punch adjustments record the old value, new value, reason, and adjuster.

The kiosk is what makes [actuals preservation on reschedule](/blog/what-is-actuals-preservation-on-reschedule) possible: the reschedule can only preserve completed work because the kiosk recorded it. Pauses are captured with a [reason code](/blog/what-is-a-reason-code-in-production-tracking) so the plant knows why a job stalled, and the [shop floor guide](/blog/edgebic-shop-floor-guide) walks through the operator's day at the terminal.
