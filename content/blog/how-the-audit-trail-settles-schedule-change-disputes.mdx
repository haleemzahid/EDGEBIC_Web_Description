**A schedule change log audit trail settles the "who moved this job" argument by recording every change with its old dates, its new dates, and the run that made it, so the answer is a two-minute lookup instead of a blame conversation.** EDGEBIC by User Solutions writes that record automatically on every scheduling run, and the Reschedule History report reads it back. The value is not the record itself. It is that the record replaces memory, and a record does not have a stake in the outcome.

## The Argument That Wastes an Afternoon

Every plant has had this conversation. A promised job slipped. Sales says production moved it. Production says the date was never firm. The supervisor thinks a rush order bumped it. Nobody is lying, and nobody can prove anything, because the evidence is three people's recollection of a plan that has changed a dozen times since.

The meeting to settle it burns an afternoon and settles nothing, because the underlying problem is that the schedule left no trace of its own history. Each reschedule overwrote the last, and the previous dates are simply gone. You cannot audit a plan that only ever shows its current state.

## What Gets Recorded, and When

The change log removes the ambiguity by recording the move as it happens. Every scheduling run that shifts a job writes an entry, and the entry carries the facts a dispute needs:

- **The job** that moved.
- **The old planned dates** it held before the run.
- **The new planned dates** it holds after.
- **The size of the shift** in working days.

Because the run itself writes the entry, the record does not depend on a planner remembering to note what they did. A reschedule after a breakdown, a manual Gantt drag, and a routine morning run all leave the same kind of trail. That automatic capture is what makes the log trustworthy: it records what happened, not what someone decided to write down. See [what is a schedule change log](/blog/what-is-a-schedule-change-log) for the underlying concept.

## Two Reports, Two Questions

The log answers the two questions disputes actually turn on, through two reports:

| Question | Report | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| When did this job's date move, and by how much? | Reschedule History | Old dates, new dates, days shifted, per run |
| Why is this job on a different machine than it was? | Resource replacement audit | Where a job was rerouted, from which resource to which |

A date-slip complaint goes to the [Reschedule History report](/blog/how-to-run-the-reschedule-history-report-in-edgebic). A "this was supposed to run on the good machine" complaint goes to the [resource replacement audit](/blog/how-to-run-the-resource-replacement-audit-in-edgebic). In both cases the planner reads a record rather than reconstructing one.

## Why This Ends the Dispute Instead of Fueling It

The reason a change log settles arguments is that it changes what the argument is about. Without a record, the conversation is "who is right," which is unwinnable because both sides remember a different plan. With a record, the conversation is "here is what the log shows, now what do we do," which is solvable.

The job moved two days on Tuesday's run because a machine was blocked: that is a fact, not an accusation. Once the fact is on the table, the productive conversation can start. Does the customer get the new date? Does a Saturday recover it? Does a different order absorb the slip? Those are decisions, and decisions need facts, which is exactly what the trail supplies. This is the enforcement layer under [how one plan aligns sales and production promises](/blog/how-one-plan-aligns-sales-and-production-promises).

## The Regulated-Shop Case

For shops under quality or contract audit, the trail is not a convenience. It is evidence of a controlled process. An auditor asking whether your schedule is managed rather than improvised wants to see that changes are captured with before-and-after values, automatically, whether or not a planner was paying attention.

That is precisely what the log provides. The heritage lineage behind EDGEBIC includes the kind of work where this matters most: US Navy overhauls coordinating more than 26,000 tasks on the USS Nimitz, and regulated manufacturing across the User Solutions install base. A plan you can prove you controlled is worth more, in those settings, than a plan that was merely correct. See [audit-ready scheduling](/blog/audit-ready-scheduling) for the compliance framing.

## What the Audit Trail Cannot Do

**It cannot tell you the change was a good decision.** The log records that a job moved two days. Whether moving it was the right call is a judgment the record informs but does not make. Accountability is about knowing what happened, not about grading it.

**It cannot record a change made outside the system.** A job someone rearranged on a whiteboard and never entered leaves no trail, because the log only sees what the software did. The record is complete for changes the software makes and blind to changes it never sees, which is one more reason to keep the plan in one place. See [how one source of truth ends spreadsheet sprawl](/blog/how-one-source-of-truth-ends-spreadsheet-sprawl).

**It cannot assign blame, and should not.** The trail shows what moved and when. It does not know whether the mover was justified, careless, or responding to an emergency. Using it as a weapon rather than a fact source defeats the purpose, which is to end the argument, not to win it.

**It cannot substitute for the conversation about what to do next.** Settling who moved the job is the start of the useful discussion, not the end of it. The record gets you to the decision faster; the decision is still yours.
