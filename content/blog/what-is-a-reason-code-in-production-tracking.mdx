**A reason code is a short, selectable label that records why a job paused or an anomaly occurred on the shop floor, such as a machine breakdown, missing material, a quality hold, or waiting on something else.** It is the drop-down an operator or planner picks when a job stops, the same idea as the code a call-center agent chooses to explain why a call ran long. A reason code turns a raw block of lost time into a categorized, reportable cause you can actually act on.

## How a Reason Code Works

When a job stops, something happened. The machine broke, the material did not arrive, an inspector put a hold on it, or the operator was blocked waiting for a decision. Recording only that the job paused loses the most useful information: the cause.

A reason code captures that cause. When a pause is recorded, the operator or planner selects a code, and that code carries a category so the pause rolls up cleanly. The standard categories are four:

- **Machine:** the pause was caused by equipment, such as broken tooling or a failed line.
- **Material:** missing or defective material stopped the work, such as no blanks on hand.
- **Quality:** an inspection or hold, such as a first-article check the job must clear.
- **Waiting:** the operator is blocked on something else, such as an engineering approval.

Specific codes sit under those categories. Broken tooling and a coolant leak both roll up under machine; a missing blank rolls up under material. That structure is what turns a week of scattered pauses into a ranked picture of where the plant is losing time.

## A Concrete Example

An operator is running a job on CNC-3 when the coolant line fails. At the kiosk they pause the job and pick a reason code: coolant failure, under the machine category. Maintenance is called, the line is fixed, and the operator resumes.

Now scale that across a month. The downtime records alone would tell you CNC-3 lost 40 hours to pauses. The reason codes tell you 28 of those hours were machine reasons, 8 were material, and 4 were quality, and that within machine, coolant and spindle faults dominate. That is the difference between knowing you lost time and knowing where to send maintenance and which spare to stock. The pause without a code is a symptom; the code is the diagnosis.

## How EDGEBIC Uses It

In EDGEBIC reason codes are attached when a job is paused, at the [shop floor kiosk](/blog/what-is-a-shop-floor-kiosk) or by a planner, and they roll up into downtime reporting.

- **A pause carries a code.** When a job stops, the operator or planner selects a reason code, so the stop is categorized rather than anonymous.
- **Codes roll up by category.** Every code sits under machine, material, quality, or waiting, so lost time can be analyzed by cause and by station.
- **The list is maintained and seeded.** Reason codes are their own maintainable list with a seeded starter set, so a plant can match its own vocabulary while keeping the categories consistent.
- **A machine-down punch pairs with a code.** At the kiosk a down punch records both how long the machine was down and, through the code, why.

Reason codes are what make shop-floor data diagnostic instead of merely quantitative: the [daily hours](/blog/what-is-a-daily-hour-breakdown) tell you how much time each day held, and the reason codes tell you why any of it was lost. For the moment a code is actually attached to a punch or a scrap count, read [what is a reason stamp in shop floor tracking](/blog/what-is-a-reason-stamp-in-shop-floor-tracking). To attach one on a real screen, see [how to pause a job with a reason code in EDGEBIC](/blog/how-to-pause-a-job-with-a-reason-code-in-edgebic), and the [shop floor guide](/blog/edgebic-shop-floor-guide) walks through the operator's terminal in full.
