**When the run sequence at a machine is decided by whoever pushes hardest, the plant pays for it twice: once in unplanned changeovers, and again in the quiet jobs that go late while the loud ones get expedited.** EDGEBIC by User Solutions replaces that with a Work Center Dispatch list ordered by planned start, so the sequence is a published result of the plan rather than a private judgment made at the machine. The change is not about authority. It is that pressure and urgency are two different things, and only one of them predicts lateness.

This post covers the sequencing outcome. It sits under the [EDGEBIC results guide](/blog/edgebic-results-guide). For how supervisors use it day to day, see [the EDGEBIC dispatch view](/blog/how-supervisors-use-the-edgebic-dispatch-view).

## Pressure Is Not Urgency

Here is the failure in one sentence: the job someone chases hardest is usually the one with the most attentive customer, not the one closest to missing its date.

Those two populations barely overlap. Attentive customers call weekly whether or not their order is at risk. Quiet customers do not call at all until the date passes. If sequence follows call volume, you systematically service the accounts that were never in danger and starve the accounts that were, and the shop's late list fills with jobs nobody was watching.

Everyone knows this and it still happens, because at the machine, in the moment, the loud request is the only information present. The dispatch list works by making better information present.

## What the List Actually Is

Work Center Dispatch is one report with a narrow job. It lists every operation whose planned window overlaps the date range you set, grouped by work center with groups auto-expanded and ordered by planned start. Top to bottom is the run sequence.

Three details make it usable at the machine rather than in an office:

- **Material-only rows are excluded.** Material does not queue at a machine, so it does not belong on a run queue.
- **Remaining Hours is required hours minus actual hours, floored at zero.** An operator picking up a half-finished operation reads what is genuinely left rather than the original estimate. That one column removes a whole class of "this says four hours but it is nearly done" confusion.
- **It defaults to today through the next 14 days**, and exports to PDF, so a station gets a page rather than a screen it has to go find.

The planned start dates behind that ordering come from the schedule, and the schedule sequences orders before it places them, driven by start and due dates. See [what a work center dispatch list is](/blog/what-is-a-work-center-dispatch-list) for the generic concept.

## The Cost of an Insertion

Sequencing by pressure is really a sequence of insertions, and insertions are expensive in a way that is easy to miss because the cost lands on other jobs.

Take a shop expediting eight jobs a week, each insertion forcing one extra changeover:

| | Per insertion | Per week | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extra changeover | 45 minutes | 6 hours | ~300 hours |
| Jobs displaced | 1 to 4 | 8 to 32 | large |

The 300 hours is the visible number. The displaced jobs are the expensive one, because each displaced job pushes its own downstream operations and becomes a candidate for next week's expedite. That is the mechanism by which expediting sustains itself: this week's insertions manufacture next week's emergencies, and the queue never settles. See [how EDGEBIC reduces expediting](/blog/how-edgebic-reduces-expediting) for the wider version of that loop.

## Where Escalation Goes Instead

The point is not to abolish rush work. Shops have genuine emergencies and a system that cannot absorb one is not usable.

What changes is where the decision happens. Under a dispatch list, moving a job up is a change to the plan, made by the planner, with the consequence visible before it is committed: you can see which jobs the insertion pushes and by how much. Under the loudest-voice model, the same decision happens at the machine, invisibly, and its consequences surface a week later as three unrelated late jobs nobody connects back to it.

| | Loudest voice | Dispatch list |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides sequence | Supervisor, under pressure | The plan, published |
| When consequences appear | Next week, as late jobs | Before the change is committed |
| Is the change recorded | No | Yes, in the change history |
| What an override tells you | Nothing | Where the plan is wrong |

That last row is worth dwelling on. A station whose supervisor overrides the list constantly is not a discipline problem. It is usually telling you the routing hours are wrong or the setup sequence is bad, and the override pattern is the fastest way to find that.

## The Delivery Number Behind It

Consistent sequencing is one of the least glamorous ways a plant improves on-time delivery and one of the most reliable, because it costs nothing to install. The heritage lineage behind EDGEBIC includes the clearest version of that result: GE Railcar moved from about 30 percent on-time to about 90 percent using the User Solutions and RMDB approach, and the mechanism was not new machines. It was planning the work against real capacity and running it in the planned order.

The same logic runs through the delivery reports here. On-Time Delivery judges finished jobs by their actual end and unfinished jobs by their scheduled end, so it doubles as a scorecard and an early-warning list. Late Jobs ranks what is behind with a blocker hint on each row. Sequence changes show up in both within a few weeks. ## What a Dispatch List Cannot Do

**It cannot decide which emergency is real.** A customer shutting down a line is genuinely different from a customer who is anxious. The list makes the cost of the insertion visible; judging whether the cost is worth paying is still yours.

**It is only as good as the plan behind it.** If routing hours are wrong or capacity is overstated, the planned starts are wrong and so is the order. Sequencing correctly against a bad plan is not an improvement. See [how variance data fixes the routing that was always wrong](/blog/how-variance-data-fixes-the-routing-that-was-always-wrong).

**It does not sequence for changeover on its own.** Ordering by planned start is a due-date behavior. When a setup-heavy line needs like-with-like grouping, that comes from the setup configuration and the sequencing decisions built on it, and the two objectives genuinely pull against each other. See [how a scheduler trades off setup against due date](/blog/how-a-scheduler-trades-off-setup-against-due-date).

**It is a printed snapshot, not a live feed.** Reports are point-in-time. A list printed at 6 a.m. does not know about the 9 a.m. breakdown, so a disrupted day still needs a reschedule and a reprint.

**It will not survive a shop that does not log actuals.** Remaining Hours depends on logged hours. Without them the column reads the same as the estimate, and the list loses the feature supervisors like most. See [job shop scheduling challenges](/blog/job-shop-scheduling-challenges) for why that habit is the hard part.
