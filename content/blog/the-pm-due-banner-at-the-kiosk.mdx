**The EDGEBIC by User Solutions kiosk shows a preventive maintenance banner on its idle screen when a work center's last service date plus its maintenance interval has arrived or is within seven days.** It writes no punch, holds no capacity, and asks for no acknowledgment. It exists to put one fact in front of the person standing at the machine before they start the next job, at the only moment when acting on it is cheap.

This is a small feature with a specific and limited job. Understanding exactly where its responsibility ends is what keeps it useful.

## Two Fields, One Derived Date

Configuring it takes about fifteen seconds per machine, in the work center editor:

- **Preventive maintenance interval days.** How often this machine is serviced, for example 30 for monthly.
- **Last preventive maintenance date.** When it was last done.

From those, the due date is calculated whenever the kiosk asks: last service date plus interval days. Nothing is stored as a separate maintenance schedule, which means correcting the last service date immediately corrects every future banner with no cleanup.

| Condition | What the operator sees |
|---|---|
| Interval not set | No banner at all |
| Due date more than seven days away | No banner |
| Due date within seven days | Approaching banner |
| Due date today or past | Overdue banner |

The seven-day approaching window is what turns the feature from an alarm into a planning prompt. A machine that will be due Friday shows its banner Monday, while there is still a week of schedule to arrange around.

## Where It Appears

The banner sits on the kiosk idle screen, the same screen that shows the next queued job, the jobs behind it, and the operator's own day so far. See [what an operator sees at the kiosk](/blog/what-an-operator-sees-at-the-kiosk).

That placement is the whole design. The idle screen is what an operator reads between jobs, which is precisely when a machine can be released for service without interrupting a run. A banner that appeared mid-run would be either ignored or actively harmful.

It shares that screen real estate with the routing drift banner, which warns when a job's routing has changed since it was scheduled. Both are non-blocking notices shown before setup begins, for the same reason: the moment before you start is the cheapest moment to learn something. See [the BOR drift banner at the kiosk](/blog/the-bor-drift-banner-at-the-kiosk).

## What It Deliberately Does Not Do

The banner does not schedule anything. EDGEBIC does not create a maintenance job, does not reserve the machine, and does not reduce available hours because a service is due. If you do nothing but read the banner, the schedule continues to plan work onto that machine as though nothing were pending.

That restraint is honest rather than lazy. Deciding when a machine actually comes out of production is a planning conversation involving the queue in front of it, the maintenance crew's availability, and how badly this week needs the capacity. A system that unilaterally blocked a bottleneck for four hours because a counter rolled over would be overridden within a week and then distrusted.

## Protecting the Time Properly

When you do commit to the service, the mechanism that protects the hours is a downtime event on that work center. A downtime event removes hours from the schedulable calendar for the window you specify, so the engine plans around it instead of onto it. Machine availability, holidays, downtime, and capacity overrides all compose into the hours a work center can actually offer, which is the same chain covered in [how EDGEBIC allocates hours across shifts](/blog/how-edgebic-allocates-hours-across-shifts).

The full sequence is therefore:

1. Kiosk shows the approaching banner. Operator mentions it to the supervisor.
2. Planner and maintenance agree on a window.
3. Planner enters a downtime event on the work center for that window.
4. The next scheduling run plans work around the gap rather than into it.
5. Service is performed.
6. Somebody updates the last maintenance date on the work center, and the banner clears.

Step six is the one that gets skipped, and skipping it is why banners go stale. Treat it as part of closing out the work, not as an afterthought.

## A Worked Example

Heat-1, the shop's bottleneck. Interval 30 days, last serviced 12 May, so the due date is 11 June.

**4 June.** The kiosk starts showing an approaching banner. The operator mentions it at the morning meeting.

**5 June.** The planner looks at Heat-1's load. It is running above 100 percent utilization for the next two weeks, so a four-hour stoppage is expensive but a failure would be far worse. Maintenance can come Thursday afternoon. The planner enters a downtime event for 11 June, 13:00 to 17:00.

**Next scheduling run.** Heat-1's available hours for 11 June drop by four. Two operations that would have landed in that window are placed elsewhere. Nothing has to be moved by hand and no operator is told at 13:00 that their job is being pulled off the machine.

**11 June, after the service.** The last maintenance date is updated to 11 June. The derived due date becomes 11 July, the banner clears, and the cycle repeats.

Because Heat-1 is the constraint, that four-hour gap was worth planning rather than discovering. On a machine running at 40 percent utilization the same decision would barely register.

## Getting Value From It

Three habits make the difference between a banner people act on and wallpaper.

**Configure only the machines that genuinely run on a calendar.** Leaving the interval unset is a valid and useful choice.

**Update the last service date every time.** A permanently overdue banner trains the floor to ignore all banners, including the ones that are true.

**Use the seven-day window as intended.** The point of an approaching banner is to give the planner a week of schedule to work with. A shop that only reacts to the overdue state has thrown that week away and will always be taking machines down at the worst moment.

## The Bottom Line

Two fields on a work center produce a derived due date, and the kiosk shows the operator an approaching or overdue banner on the idle screen before the next job starts. It never blocks work or reserves capacity: protecting the hours is a downtime event a planner enters deliberately. Keep the last service date current, act on the approaching state, and the banner becomes a quiet prompt that keeps unplanned failures off your bottleneck.
