**To schedule a job backward from its due date in EDGEBIC, set its direction to Backward, give it a real due date, leave the cut off date at today, and run the scheduler.** The job comes out right-aligned: its last operation finishes just in time and the slack sits in front of it. Everything else about the run is unchanged, because both directions use the same capacity rules in EDGEBIC by User Solutions.

This is the procedure post. For what backward scheduling is and when to prefer it, read [backward scheduling explained](/blog/edgebic-backward-scheduling-explained) first, and [forward versus backward scheduling](/blog/forward-vs-backward-scheduling) for the general comparison.

## Before You Start

| Prerequisite | Why |
|---|---|
| The order has a real due date | Backward means finish by that date. Without one the job quietly schedules forward, and the order dialog requires it when the direction is Backward. |
| The product's lead time is correct | The delivery tail is reserved out of the backward plan. A wrong value shifts the whole job. |
| You know how to run a scheduling pass | Backward is a per-order option on the ordinary run, covered in [how to run the scheduler](/blog/how-to-run-the-scheduler-in-edgebic). |

## Step 1: Set the Site Default (Optional)

If most of your work is make-to-order against promised dates, set the default once:

1. Open **Options** and go to the **Schedule** tab.
2. Under **Default Scheduling Direction**, choose **Backward**, described as scheduling each job latest-feasible so it finishes at or before its due date.
3. Under **When a Backward Job Doesn't Fit**, choose between accepting the forward schedule silently and showing a popup for review.
4. Save.

The default only pre-selects the direction on **new** orders and quotes. Existing orders are untouched, and every order can still be switched individually. The doesn't-fit setting is read fresh on every run, so a change takes effect on the very next pass with no restart.

## Step 2: Set the Direction on One Order

1. Create or edit the order and set **Scheduling Direction** to **Backward**.
2. Two things change in the dialog. **Due Date** becomes required, marked with an asterisk, because it is now the finish-by commitment. The **Start Date** label flips to **Cut off Date**.
3. Leave the cut off date at today unless material or approvals genuinely force a later floor.

That relabelling carries real meaning. On a backward job the date is not "start here", it is "do not start before". It is a floor, and no operation of the job will be placed earlier than it. An artificially late floor is the single most common cause of a backward job failing to fit, because it squeezes the window the engine has to place work in.

The direction column's tooltip is the one-line summary worth memorising: forward schedules from the start date, backward finishes by the due date, and the field locks once actuals are logged.

## Step 3: Run the Scheduler

Run **Schedule + Re-Schedule** exactly as you would for any job. Nothing about the procedure changes. The job comes back right-aligned, with its last operation ending just in time and empty days visible in front of it.

Those empty days are the point. Do not drag work into them.

## Step 4: Read the Result

Three columns tell the story:

- **Item Start** is when the last work center operation ends.
- **Job End** is Item Start plus the product's lead time: the delivery-ready date.
- **Days Late** compares Job End against the due date. A backward job that fitted reads zero.

On the Gantt you will also see a muted band after the last operation, captioned with the lead time in days. It is display only: it consumes no capacity, cannot be dragged or resized, and nothing schedules into it.

## A Worked Backward Run

Fifty brackets. Direction Backward. Cut off date Thursday July 16 (today). Due Wednesday July 22. Routing: saw 2 hours, then mill 8 hours. Product lead time 1 day. One day shift, 08:00 to 16:00, no other load.

1. **The target.** Due Wednesday means delivery-ready by the end of Wednesday. Reserving the one day tail, all machining must finish by the end of Tuesday July 21.
2. **Last step first.** The 8 hour mill run is right-aligned to that limit: Tuesday July 21, 08:00 to 16:00.
3. **Feeding it.** The 2 hour cut must end by Tuesday 08:00. Right-aligned, that is Monday July 20, 14:00 to 16:00.
4. **The result.** Item Start Tuesday 16:00, Job End Wednesday July 22, Days Late 0. On the Gantt: empty space from Thursday through Monday at 14:00, two tight bars, then the lead time band ending on the due date.

Compare that with the same order scheduled forward, which would run the cut Thursday morning and finish machining days earlier. Same hours, same machines, opposite end of the window.

## When It Does Not Fit

Change one thing: the customer wants it Friday July 17, tomorrow. Machining would have to finish by the end of Thursday July 16 after reserving the one day tail, and only one eight hour shift exists between the cut off and that limit. The routing needs ten sequential hours.

The backward attempt is abandoned completely, with no half-placed operations left behind, and the whole order is scheduled forward instead: saw Thursday 08:00 to 10:00, mill Thursday 10:00 to 16:00 and Friday 08:00 to 10:00. Work ends Friday at 10:00, Job End is Saturday July 18, and the job is one day late.

What you see next depends on your Options setting.

**With Accept the forward schedule (the default)**, the run saves immediately and the job carries its projected lateness on the grid. Check the Days Late column after runs.

**With Show a popup**, the run computes everything and saves nothing, then shows the jobs that did not fit:

- One row per affected job, with a reason line explaining why it did not fit.
- Columns for **Due Date**, **Start Date** and **Priority**, all editable in place.
- A **forward start to finish** column showing the complete fallback window, so you see where the job would begin as well as when it would end.
- A status pill reading on time in green, or late by a number of days in red.

Three buttons:

| Button | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Accept Forward Schedule** | Saves the held run as computed, late where shown |
| **Adjust and Re-run** | Saves your edited dates and priority onto the orders, discards the held run, and recomputes |
| **Cancel** | Discards everything. The database is untouched, as if the run never happened |

Two scope rules keep this predictable. The popup fires only during a Drive Schedule run, and only for **new** backward jobs. Reschedules of already-planned jobs never trigger it, because backward applies to a job's first schedule only.

## After the Run: Four Things to Check

| Check | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| **Days Late** | Zero on every backward job that fitted. A value here means the fallback fired |
| **Item Start and Job End** | Job End lands on or before the due date, with the tail accounted for |
| **The front slack** | Empty days before the job, which is the plan working as intended |
| **The failures list** | Empty, or every entry understood and acted on |

If a backward job came back with lateness and you were not prompted, the doesn't-fit setting is on the silent option. That is the first thing to change once backward jobs carry real promises.

## Backward on a Quote

The same direction choice exists on a quote, and it changes what the requested date means. On a forward quote, the requested date is treated as a target start. On a backward quote it is the customer's finish-by date, and the simulation answers how late you could start and still deliver on the day.

Two things carry across. The direction travels with the quote when it converts to an order, so a backward quote becomes a backward job. And the simulation respects capacity already committed to live work, which is what makes the resulting promise date something you can defend rather than a best case. The wider workflow is in the [EDGEBIC quoting guide](/blog/edgebic-quoting-guide).

## One Warning Worth Repeating

**Never pin a target start date on a backward job.** Pinning one promotes the job to an anchored job, which outranks the backward setting, and it stops being backward at all. This is precisely why the popup's start date edits change only the ordinary start floor: the safe lever is the cut off date, not a pin.

## If It Keeps Not Fitting, Compute the Window by Hand

When a job refuses to fit and the reason is not obvious, do the arithmetic yourself once. It takes two minutes and it settles the question.

Count the **working hours available**: from the cut off date to the due date minus the product's lead time, using the shifts that actually run on the machines in the routing. Then count the **working hours needed**: every operation's run time plus setup, plus every queue time and transit day between them, added in sequence rather than in parallel.

If the second number is larger, no scheduler can fit the job and the popup is telling the truth. The levers are then real ones: move the due date, pull the cut off date in, raise the priority so the job wins contested capacity, add a shift, or split the order. If the first number is larger and it still does not fit, the cause is contention rather than arithmetic, and the priority column is where to look.
