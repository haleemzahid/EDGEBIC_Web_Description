**Pieces per hour is a work center's rated output speed: how many units it produces in one hour of running.** In [EDGEBIC by User Solutions](/edgebic), this is the figure that lets a station be scheduled in units rather than in clock time. It is combined with the work center's efficiency factor to produce an effective rate, and the effective rate is what the plan is built on.

The everyday version: a copier rated at forty pages a minute that reliably turns out thirty-six. Forty is what the box says. Thirty-six is what you should count on when you promise someone their handouts.

For the broader vocabulary of planning, see the [manufacturing glossary](/blog/manufacturing-glossary).

## How Pieces Per Hour Works

Most scheduling is described in hours. A routing step says how many hours per unit an operation takes, multiply by quantity, add setup, and you have the claim on a machine.

That framing suits machining and assembly. It fits some equipment badly. A packaging line is not naturally described as hours per bag; it is described as bags per hour. A press is described in strokes. For those stations, output is the honest unit and hours are a derived quantity.

Pieces per hour is the rate that makes the honest description possible. It says what the station produces per hour of running, and from that the engine can work out how long a quantity takes rather than being told directly.

Two figures matter here, and confusing them is the most common mistake with this setting.

The **rated** speed is the nameplate: what the equipment does when material is good, the crew is up to speed, and nothing interrupts. It is a real number and it is not a planning number.

The **effective** rate is the rated speed multiplied by the work center's efficiency factor, which is the deliberate acknowledgment that nameplate and reality differ. A line rated at five thousand bags per hour that delivers forty-five hundred over a shift is running at ninety percent, and forty-five hundred is the figure a schedule should assume.

Separately, the work center's capacity type decides what the station is scheduled against at all: hours, pieces, or both. Setting a rate does not by itself switch a station to pieces-based scheduling. If both limits apply, whichever binds first constrains the plan, which suits equipment with a cycle limit and a second physical limit such as tonnage.

## A Concrete Example

Packaging line, rated five thousand bags per hour. Job 42 needs sixty thousand bags packed.

On the rated figure that is twelve hours, comfortably inside two shifts. The plan says the job clears Tuesday.

The line does not sustain five thousand. Across a real shift, with changeovers, a jam or two, and the ramp at the start of each run, it averages forty-five hundred. Its efficiency factor is set to ninety percent, so its effective rate is forty-five hundred bags per hour.

Sixty thousand bags at forty-five hundred an hour is thirteen and a third hours, not twelve. On this one job the difference is under an hour and a half, which feels tolerable.

It is not tolerable, because it is not one job. Every job through that line is planned about eleven percent short. Across a week the shortfalls accumulate into a station that is quietly overbooked, work that spills into the next shift, and downstream operations whose start times were never real. The line is not underperforming; the plan was written against a number the line never claimed to deliver.

Scheduling on the effective rate makes the same line finish when the board says it will.

## How EDGEBIC Uses It

Pieces per hour is a work center setting used by the capacity calculation:

- **It gives a station an output rate**, so equipment naturally measured in units can be planned in units rather than in inferred hours.
- **It is combined with the efficiency factor** to produce an effective rate, which is the figure the plan is built on rather than the nameplate speed.
- **It works with the capacity type**, which decides whether a station is scheduled on hours, on pieces, or on both with the binding limit constraining the plan.
